The companion paper, retold

When Yes Becomes a Number

The same warehouse, with one thing changed: the entries in the ledger stop being yes and no, and start being quantities that can cancel each other out. What comes out the other side is quantum computing.

What this is

This retells the second paper, What Survives the Semiring, the way the previous document retold the first. It is a sequel in the strict sense: it assumes the warehouse of Running the Warehouse Backwards — the procedures, the manifests, the bins, the shortlists — and changes exactly one thing about it. Read that one first.

The change sounds like a technicality and is not. In the first language, the meaning of a step is a table of yes/no entries: can this consignment lead to that one? Replace those yes/no entries with quantities that have a direction, so that two routes arriving at the same place can reinforce or cancel, and the very same procedures become the operations of a quantum computer. Same text. Same reader. Same manifest checker. Literally the same reversal routine.

Start with Part zero. The paper assumes you already know what a quantum computer is for, what makes it different from an ordinary one, and why reversibility is compulsory rather than a design choice — so it never explains any of them. Part zero is not in the paper and does all three, in about five minutes. Nothing after it will make much sense without it.

The paper's actual subject is not that this works. It is what it costs, itemised. Of nineteen things you could write in the classical language, twelve survive untouched, five stop being writable, and two new ones are added. And the five that go, go for exactly two reasons, one each for two groups. That accounting is the paper.

Part zero  ·  not in the paperWhat a quantum computer is actually doing

The paper is written for people who already know this, so it never says any of it. Three things need to be on the table first: what the machine is for, the single trick it has, and why that trick makes reversibility compulsory rather than optional. Once those are down, every constraint in the rest of the document arrives already explained.

0.1What it is for

Much narrower than the press suggests. Not "computers, but faster". A short list of specific jobs where a quantum machine is known to beat every classical method:

Factoring

Given a 600-digit number that is two large primes multiplied together, find the primes. Nobody knows how to do this classically in any reasonable time — and essentially all internet security rests on that fact. A quantum machine can. This is Shor's algorithm, and it is why governments fund the field.

Searching a haystack

A million possibilities, one right answer, no structure to exploit. Classically: a million checks. Quantum: about a thousand. A square-root saving — genuinely useful, not revolutionary.

Simulating matter

The original motivation, from Feynman in 1981. Predicting how a molecule behaves means tracking something whose possibilities multiply with every atom added, and that multiplication is exactly the bill a classical computer cannot pay. So use a quantum system to simulate a quantum system. Probably the first application to matter commercially.

That is close to the whole list. Your spreadsheet will not be faster.

0.2The one trick — and what the arrow is for

Here is the part that is almost always explained wrongly, and getting it right is the whole reason the arrows in this document exist.

Yes, a quantum machine can send one consignment down a million routes at once. On its own that is worth precisely nothing. Because when you look at the result, you see one route, picked at random. A million-route machine that then picks one at random is a machine that guesses. You could do that with a die.

And notice that an ordinary computer that flips coins has exactly the same structure: a spread of possible outcomes, each carrying a weight, and the weight is a probability. But probabilities are always positive and they always accumulate. Two routes arriving at the same outcome make it more likely, never less. Nothing is ever removed. That is why a coin-flipping computer gains you nothing you could not have got by being systematic.

The entire difference

Quantum weights can cancel. Two routes arriving at the same outcome can leave nothing at all. That is the only difference between a quantum computer and a classical one, and every advantage quantum machines have traces back to it.

So: picture each weight not as a plain number but as an arrow — some length, pointing in some direction. When two routes arrive at the same outcome you do not add two numbers, you lay two arrows end to end. Pointing the same way, you get a longer arrow: that outcome is now more likely. Pointing opposite ways, you get nothing: that outcome has become impossible, precisely because two routes lead to it. Which is a sentence that makes no sense at all about probabilities, and is the whole business here.

Which gives the strategy behind every quantum algorithm ever written:

What a quantum algorithm is

Arrange the layout so that the routes leading to wrong answers cancel each other out, and the routes leading to the right answer reinforce. Then look.

The parallelism is the cheap part and achieves nothing by itself. All the engineering is in the cancellation. Grover's search, above, is exactly this: it does not check a million things, it spends about a thousand steps steadily cancelling the wrong ones.

A demonstration small enough to hold in your head

Take one lane. A lane has two bins, called bin 0 and bin 1, and the consignment sits in one of them — a lane is the smallest thing a quantum machine is built from, and physicists call it a qubit. So a lane is never "in" a bin; it has the two bins, and the goods are in one.

Start the consignment in bin 0. Now there is a standard piece of equipment — call it the splitter — which halves whatever arrives:

That single backward arrow is the entire content of the splitter — and, in a sense the rest of this document exists to justify, very nearly the entire content of quantum computing. Now send a consignment through two splitters in a row, starting in bin 0:

You end up back in bin 0 with certainty. Two splitters make nothing happen at all.

Now compare a coin. Flip it twice and you are at fifty-fifty — the second flip cannot undo the first, because probabilities only ever pile up. The splitter is not a coin. Two of them return you exactly where you started, and the reason is visible in the table: one route into bin 1 carried a backward arrow and destroyed the other.

Why the same machine turns up at both ends of an algorithm

Open any description of a quantum algorithm and you will find a splitter at the start and another at the finish, with the interesting part in between. There is a plain reason, and the demonstration you have just stepped through contains all of it.

The mismatch the sandwich exists to bridge

All the work happens in arrow directions — cancelling is the only thing this machine can do that an ordinary one cannot.

All the reading sees only which bin. Directions are invisible to it: turn every arrow by the same amount and no measurement anywhere can tell.

So the computation is done in a currency the read-out will not accept, and something has to convert. The splitter is that converter, and you have just watched it convert. Two arrows arriving in bin 1 pointing the same way sent you to bin 0; pointing opposite ways they would have sent you to bin 1. The second splitter took a question you cannot ask — are these two arrows aligned or opposed? — and turned it into one you can: which bin?

That is the closing splitter: an instrument for reading arrow directions. The opening one does the mirror job. You can only ever prepare a definite bin, and a definite bin means exactly one route — and one route has nothing to interfere with. Cancelling needs at least two routes whose arrows can be set against each other, so before anything can happen you must spread the goods across the bins. Same instrument, other direction.

The cleanest way to hold it

A splitter changes the question the bins are answering. Before it, bin 0 versus bin 1 means "which bin". After it, the same two bins mean "were the arrows aligned or opposed".

Which is also why doing it twice does nothing: you have asked for the original question back. An algorithm needs one question to compute in and a different one to read the answer in — so it gets a converter at each end.

Deutsch's algorithm is the smallest real case. You are handed a sealed question and asked whether it gives the same answer for both its inputs — a question about a relationship, not about either answer, which is why classically you must ask it twice.

  1. Opening splitter. Spread across both bins, so both inputs go to the sealed question on a single run.
  2. The sealed question writes its two answers into the arrow directions: the route through one bin keeps its arrow, the route through the other is turned to point backwards or not, according to whether the two answers differ.
  3. So what you wanted to know is now exactly are these two arrows aligned or opposed?
  4. Closing splitter. Converts precisely that into a bin. Aligned puts you in bin 0, opposed in bin 1, with certainty — and you asked once.

It is not only Deutsch. Grover's search wraps a simple reflection in splitters; the Fourier layout of §4.2.1 opens each of its stages with one. Wherever a computation is natural to express in one currency and its answer only readable in the other, converters appear at both ends.

One line worth keeping: the demonstration above is a complete quantum algorithm with nothing in the middle. Put in between the two splitters a step that turns one arrow to point backwards — §4 calls it Z — and instead of bin 0 you land in bin 1, with certainty. The machine has detected that a sign was inserted, which no amount of looking at the state in the middle could have told you. That is one of the identities §6 checks by running it, read the other way round, and it is the entire shape of a quantum algorithm in three steps.

Where else can an arrow point?

Everywhere. The demonstration above used only two directions, forward and backward, and that was an honest simplification rather than the whole truth: those are simply the only two the splitter ever produces. An arrow is free to point anywhere on a full circle, and reaching the directions in between is what most of the equipment in §4 exists to do.

So cancellation is a matter of degree rather than an all-or-nothing affair. Two arrows exactly opposed and of equal length leave nothing behind; two identical ones reinforce fully; anything in between partly reinforces. Algorithms aim squarely at the two extremes, because you want the wrong answers dead rather than merely dimmed — a dimmed wrong answer is still an answer you might read when you look.

§4 puts labels on that circle. It turns out the positions have familiar names, and that the language can reach only eight of them.

Hold on to this, because it recurs. The splitter is the station everyone calls Hadamard, and it is the most used piece of equipment in quantum computing: it turns a definite arrangement into a spread, and a spread back into a definite answer, which is why it appears at both ends of nearly every algorithm. That two of them make the do-nothing procedure is one of the identities the tool in §6 checks by running it.

0.3Why looking is special

Everything above is bookkeeping on arrows. Looking is the one operation that is not, and it behaves quite unlike anything in ordinary computing:

  • You see exactly one outcome, never the spread.
  • Which one is a matter of chance, weighted by the square of each arrow's length. Long arrow, likely. No arrow, impossible.
  • And afterwards the rest is gone. The spread does not survive being looked at. From then on, the lane simply holds what you saw.

So you get one look, and you do not get to peek along the way. Peeking is not merely inconvenient: an arrow you have already observed can no longer cancel against anything, so peeking destroys the very cancellation you spent the whole layout setting up. This is why the paper is so careful about where looking is allowed to happen (§7), and why it turns out that confining it to the very end costs nothing.

An "observer", incidentally, need not be a person. Any interaction that lets information about which route was taken escape into something you do not control counts as a look — a single stray photon will do it. The rule is about information leaking, not about consciousness.

Yes, this is Schrödinger's cat

It is exactly the same rule, and three things about the famous version are worth knowing.

The cat was a complaint, not an explanation. Schrödinger proposed it in 1935 to argue that the standard interpretation was ridiculous — he was on Einstein's side of that argument. The point was: if you take collapse-on-observation seriously you are committed to a cat smeared across alive and dead until someone opens the box, and since that is absurd, something must be wrong. He complained that it is one thing to have a blurry photograph and quite another to have a sharp photograph of a cloud. The popular use of the cat as "this is how it works" inverts what it was for.

What really ends the cat's spread is its size, not the looking. A cat is in contact with something like ten trillion trillion air molecules, plus its own body heat and every passing photon. The arrows do not stay in the cat; they leak into all of that, essentially instantly. And once they are spread across that many particles you would have to gather every one of them back in exact alignment to make anything cancel again. So the cat is definite long before anyone opens the box. Nobody has to look — the environment is already looking, everywhere, continuously.

That is not a digression. It is the reason quantum computers are hard to build. The whole engineering problem is stopping the environment from looking: refrigerators a hair above absolute zero, shielding, and a coherence budget that the layout spends down as it runs. It is also why §7.3 exists — a lane whose look has been postponed is still sitting there, leaking.

Whether the collapse is fundamentally irreversible is genuinely unsettled, and the question has a name: the measurement problem. On the textbook view it is a real, random, irreversible event. On another mainstream view nothing ever collapses at all — everything stays reversible forever, and what you call collapse is your branch of events losing the ability to interfere with the others, irreversible only in the way an egg is: not forbidden, merely so improbable that it never happens. There are other positions still.

Happily, none of that matters here. The mainstream interpretations agree on every prediction anyone can currently test, and for a person building or programming one of these machines looking is irreversible and chancy under all of them. This document, and the paper, make claims about what a programmer can do — not about what reality is — so they hold either way.

One last connection, for later. The theorem in §7.2 — that you never have to look partway through, because you can keep everything in the spread and look at the end — is a small, precise, engineering-grade version of exactly this. In the textbook telling of teleportation, Alice looks at her two lanes and telephones the result. The language of this paper says: do not open the box. Keep it all entangled, use quantum control instead, and look once at the end.

0.4Why reversibility is compulsory

Now the question behind the whole project: why is a paper about reversible programming relevant to quantum computing at all? There are two answers, and they arrive from completely different directions.

The physical answer

The law governing an isolated quantum system says its evolution is invertible: the state now determines the state at every earlier moment. That is not a design goal or a convention — it is what the equation says. So every station in a quantum layout must be reversible. There is no "erase this lane" instruction, because physics does not supply one. The only irreversible thing in the entire business is looking, which is exactly why looking has to sit outside.

The older answer, about heat

Reversible computing is decades older than quantum computing and started from thermodynamics. Landauer, 1961: erasing one bit of information necessarily releases a definite minimum quantity of heat — not in practice, but necessarily. Bennett, 1973: therefore a computation that never erases anything has, in principle, no minimum energy cost at all. That is the founding argument of the field the companion paper belongs to, and it has nothing to do with quantum mechanics.

So there are two entirely independent reasons to want a language in which nothing may be thrown away. And here is the pleasing part: the language in the companion paper was built for neither of them. It came from machine translation — grammars that had to run in both directions, turning a sentence into a meaning and a meaning back into a sentence. A third road to the same constraint.

Which is why this paper is possible at all

A language designed, for reasons having nothing to do with physics, under exactly the constraint that physics imposes — turns out, when you change what is written in its ledger, to be a quantum language. Not a language resembling one. The same file, handed to a different reader.

0.5So what does a quantum program look like?

Put all of the above together and the shape of the thing is forced:

  • A fixed number of lanes, decided before you start — the machine is a physical object with a fixed number of them.
  • A fixed sequence of stations wired across those lanes. No loops. No branches. No "if". A layout, drawn once, that goods flow through.
  • Every station reversible, because there is no other kind.
  • One look, at the very end.

Two words for one thing, while we are here: a step is what you write in a procedure, and a station is that same step standing on the floor with goods passing through it. The document uses whichever fits the sentence.

Which explains, in advance, four things the paper spends its length on. Why losing the "repeat" construct is survivable rather than fatal — a layout was always going to be a fixed, finite thing (§4.2). Why there must nevertheless be a family of layouts, one per job size, and something to generate them (§4.2.1). Why looking lives outside the language (§7). And why every single step has to conserve everything exactly (§3.3).

From here on, the paper.

AbstractThe argument in one page

There are two known ways to get from a reversible warehouse language to quantum computing. One adds machinery: take two copies of the language, bolt them together with an interface layer, introduce a special "blank" and a special "check it is blank", and impose a compatibility rule. That route works, and it lands you in a world where a step is allowed to quietly lose some of the consignment.

This paper takes the other way: change what the ledger entries are. Read the very same procedures with quantities-that-cancel instead of yes/no, add two new basic steps, and you have a fully general quantum language that stays in the world where every step conserves everything.

The interest is the bill, and the bill is exact. Of nineteen constructs, twelve survive unchanged, two are added, five are lost — and the five fall into two groups under two independent rules. Two of them go because they were defined using "yes or yes is still yes", which is false of quantities. Three of them go because they fail a conservation test. Everything else — including the reversal operator, the reader, and the entire manifest system — is not merely analogous but the same code: the reversal routine of the quantum language is the same routine as the classical one, and one file of definitions runs under both readers unmodified.

Losing "repeat" costs the language its only loop, and the paper shows what replaces it: the family of layouts that quantum computing actually asks for is generated by the classical twin — and because the generator is itself a reversible program, it runs backwards. The paper also shows the rulebook is runnable rather than merely asserted, and that looking at the result can be added at the edge without touching the language, along with a compiler stage, which no previous language needs, that undoes what the design forces.

Section 1Two ways to get here

A reversible warehouse language and a quantum computer look, from a distance, like different subjects. Structurally they are not, and the reason is worth stating carefully because everything else follows from it.

The meaning of a procedure in the first language is a grid. Down the side, every consignment that could go in; across the top, every consignment that could come out; and in each cell, a yes or a no — can this lead to that? Composing two procedures is the obvious grid arithmetic: to get from A to C, check every possible midpoint.

That grid is what this document calls a procedure's ledger. The two words are one thing: the grid is its shape, the ledger is what its cells hold, and everything that follows turns on what they are allowed to hold.

Now put numbers in the cells instead of yes and no. Nothing else changes: the same grid, the same arithmetic, the same "check every midpoint". But the numbers can be negative, or more generally can point in different directions, and so two routes that arrive at the same destination can now cancel out completely instead of both counting. Grids of that kind, with the conservation rule of §3.3, are exactly the operations of a quantum computer.

The observation the paper takes literally

A language whose basic steps are just the rules for shuffling bins and pairs around does not, from its own text, know which kind of grid it is being read with.

So the same file is handed to two different readers. Here is one, in full:

Read with quantities, the same six words are one of the standard gates of quantum computing. Same text, same parser, same manifest inference, same reverse. And the sharing is not a family resemblance — in the working implementation, the reversal routine of the quantum language is the identical piece of code as the classical one.

1.1The other route, for contrast

This is not the first way anyone has got from a language like this to quantum computing, and the contrast is the clearest way to say what this paper does.

Add machinery

Take two copies of the language, turned relative to one another. Bolt them together with an interface layer so their procedures interleave. Add a second layer introducing a blank and a "check this is blank" operation. Impose a compatibility rule between the two copies. Their theorem is that this suffices — and the cost is stated openly: the resulting world is one of partial steps, where a step may lose part of the consignment.

Change the arithmetic

One copy. No interface layer. No imposed rule. Read the existing text with quantities instead of yes/no and add two basic steps. The world stays one in which every step conserves everything exactly. Full generality comes from an existing result: this plumbing plus two extra steps, subject to three equations, is enough for all of quantum computing.

Neither route follows from the other, and the difference is not cosmetic: it decides which world you end up in, which in turn decides what looking at the result costs you (§7). One shared casualty is worth recording. Both routes lose the "either/or" machinery, for entirely unrelated reasons. In the other route it simply is not carried across the interface layer. Here it is lost because it was defined using an arithmetic fact that is no longer true (§3.2). Same casualty, different cause.

1.2What the paper contributes

  1. The boundary, with its two reasons (§3). Nineteen constructs, twelve surviving, five lost, two added — and a statement of why each is lost that separates two independent causes. The paper knows of no other place where the bill for the classical-to-quantum move is itemised item by item within one language.
  2. A runnable rulebook (§6). The identities that are normally proved on paper — that two of a certain step cancel out, that a controlled step's reverse is the controlled reverse — are things you can execute. The tool checks them across a whole library.
  3. A layout family, generated by the classical twin (§4.2.1). The quantum language has no loop, so the plan for a job of size n has to be written somewhere else; here that somewhere is the classical language, using only an ordinary self-referential manifest and an ordinary recursive definition. The paper generates a standard, widely used layout this way — exact at three lanes, 98.1% faithful at four. And because the generator is a reversible program, the generator runs backwards: it reads a layout and tells you the job size that produced it, which no plan-builder written in an ordinary language can do.
  4. Looking, at the edge, and a stage that undoes the deferral (§7). Looking at the result needs no change to the language, because it is not a step — it acts on the finished output. Looking partway through is refused, and a theorem says that costs no expressive power; but the deferral is not free on real hardware, so the paper describes a compiler stage that reverses it, which no previous language needs because none is forced to defer in the first place.

Section 2The classical warehouse, briefly

Only what is needed. Manifests are built the same four ways — nothing qualifies, exactly one possibility, either-A-or-B, A-and-B-together — and may refer to themselves. Procedures are built from thirteen basic steps, which are the rules saying those four builders behave like arithmetic, plus copying and bin-merging; and six ways of combining them: then, two bins side by side, travelling together, either, repeat, and backwards.

Two properties carry over and matter here. Reversal always works, needing no checks, so it is pushed down to the individual steps and never appears in the machinery. And the law of the language reads: x is a possible input for y exactly when y is a possible output for x — with "possible", because running forwards and then backwards returns a shortlist containing where you started, not where you started. Both properties survive. The second is transformed, in a way that is §3.4 and the nicest single fact in the paper.

2.1What "reading it differently" actually means

This deserves an operational statement, because it is what the software does rather than a metaphor.

The classical reader

Give it a consignment; it hands back the set of consignments that could result. Combining steps means taking unions, chaining, tagging by bin, pairing.

The quantum reader

Give it a consignment; it hands back a spread: a list of possible results, each carrying an arrow — the arrows of §0.2, whose only purpose is that they can cancel. Combining steps means the same chaining, tagging and pairing — but where the classical reader takes a union, this one lays the arrows end to end.

The whole difference

The two readers walk the procedure in exactly the same way, step for step. What differs is what they do when two routes arrive at the same place. The classical reader writes down "reachable" and moves on. The quantum reader lays the two arrows end to end — which may give a longer arrow, or, if they point opposite ways, nothing at all.

That second case is the one worth the whole paper. It is the only thing a quantum computer can do that a classical one cannot (§0.2), and it is the entire reason for changing what sits in the ledger.

And that is the only place the change shows up. The written procedure never mentions what an entry in the ledger is: it is built from bin-and-pair rearrangements combined four ways, and none of the four asks what kind of thing is in a cell. Numbers appear only when the reader combines two routes. Which is why the language never had to be redesigned — there was nothing in it to redesign.

Section 3The boundary

3.1What survives

A step survives if, read with quantities, it obeys the conservation rule. Eight of the thirteen do, for one uniform reason: each of them merely rearranges. Nothing is created, nothing is lost, each definite arrangement goes to exactly one definite arrangement, and every arrangement is reached by exactly one. A pure shuffle conserves everything no matter what the ledger entries are. (Eight steps plus the four ways of combining them is the "twelve constructs" of the heading below. §3.6 says why it is exactly these eight and not some other eight.)

The manifest system is shared word for word: the rules are the same rules, "a two-bin choice" is a name for an existing manifest rather than a new kind of thing, and the inference is the same algorithm running with a different table of basic steps plugged in.

3.2What is lost, reason one: adding must not double

Two constructs are defined by accumulating possibilities. "Either procedure may apply" means: take everything the first can do and everything the second can do, and put them together. "Repeat any number of times" means the same thing, accumulated over doing it once, twice, three times, and so on.

With yes/no entries, accumulating is harmless, because yes-or-yes is still yes. Listing the same route twice in a manifest changes nothing. With quantities, listing the same route twice gives you twice as much. So:

“Either t or t”

Should obviously mean the same as "t". With quantities it means two of t — you have conjured a second consignment out of nothing. The construct no longer means anything one wants it to mean.

“Repeat t”

Was defined as the accumulation over all the finite repetitions, and that accumulation settles down to an answer only because listing something twice is harmless. With quantities the sequence need not settle at all.

So these two are not dropped for convenience. They are exactly the two constructs whose very definition uses a property the new arithmetic does not have. And note what this means for the usual intuition: one might expect quantum superposition to arrive as some form of "either/or". It does not. Choice is the thing that had to go. Superposition arrives from somewhere else entirely (§4).

The literature met this from the other side. The authors of the other route list, as future work, extending their construction with a repetition operator, and judge that doing so "would require answering fundamental open questions about the nature of infinite-dimensional quantum computation". The obstruction here is much more elementary and comes first: repetition was defined using yes-or-yes-is-yes, so it does not survive the change of arithmetic at all, whatever anyone later decides about the deeper question.

3.3What is lost, reason two: conservation

Five basic steps fail for a completely unrelated reason. Here is the conservation rule in full, and it has two halves that must both hold:

The conservation rule

(a) The step must not lose or duplicate anything: the total going in equals the total coming out.

(b) The step must be able to reach every arrangement: nothing in the outbound warehouse is unreachable.

Both halves, or the step is not allowed. And the interesting part is that the five failures fail in three distinguishable ways.

The step that never applies

Fails everything. In the classical world "no outcome" was a perfectly respectable answer — that is what let the first paper say partiality is not failure. Here there is no such step and no such consolation.

Merging the two bins

Fails (a). Two different inbound arrangements land on the same outbound one, so the outbound total is doubled and there is no way back. This was the sole source of shortlists in the classical language — and here there are no shortlists for it to be the source of.

Copying; putting into a bin

Fails (b) only. Nothing is lost going forward. But their outputs occupy only part of the outbound warehouse. This group deserves the rest of the section.

The one everybody gets wrong

Ask anyone why a quantum language cannot have a copy operation and you will be told about the famous rule that quantum states cannot be cloned. That is not the reason, and the paper is firm about it.

The no-cloning rule forbids a machine that copies an arbitrary consignment, including one already spread across several routes with arrows — and it is a real prohibition, because a machine that could duplicate a spread could be used to read it without disturbing it, which §0.3 says is impossible. The copy step here never does that. It only ever duplicates definite arrangements — and duplicating a definite arrangement is entirely permitted. It is, in fact, precisely what physicists mean by choosing a basis to read things in. Copying passes half (a) of the conservation rule perfectly.

What it fails is half (b). Run it over every possible inbound consignment and look at what comes out: matching pairs, and only matching pairs. A mismatched pair is never produced by anything. A whole region of the outbound warehouse is unreachable, so there is nothing to run backwards from there, so there is no genuine reverse.

The correction

Copying is excluded for not reaching everywhere, not for cloning. The same goes for the two bin operations: "put it in the left bin" never produces anything in the right bin.

The second half of the conservation rule is the one doing the work, and it is not any of the physical prohibitions it is easy to mistake it for. Conserving the total is not enough.

The two failures are mirror images. Lane-merging fails going one way; copying and the bin operations fail going the other. Only the second kind is easy to misread as a law of physics.

3.4What changes meaning: the shortlist collapses

One construct survives in the text and is transformed in meaning: backwards. In the classical world it is the reverse of a table of possibilities. Here it is the reverse of a table of arrows. The implementation does not change — it is the same routine — because the written text only ever manipulates the laws reversal obeys: reverse the order of a chain, distribute over the two builders, and reversing twice gets you back.

And now the most satisfying fact in the paper. The classical language's law contained the word "possible", because running forwards and then backwards returned a list containing where you started. Here, every procedure conserves everything, so:

Why this is not a coincidence

The constructs that could ever produce a shortlist — bin-merging above all — are precisely the ones that failed the conservation rule and had to go. The price the classical language paid for declining uniqueness is refunded, in full, by a change of number system. And the refund is exactly what makes the quantum reading interesting.

3.5The boundary, stated

Proposition 1

A construct of the classical language survives the change of arithmetic exactly when it is neither (i) defined by accumulating possibilities, nor (ii) in breach of the conservation rule. The constructs failing (i) are exactly either and repeat; those failing (ii) are exactly the never-applies step, bin-merging, copying, and the two bin operations. Everything else is read with quantities without modification, and backwards is reinterpreted from one kind of reverse to the other.

Twelve survive, five are lost, and §4 adds two.

3.5.1The boundary is not a matter of grammar

You would expect the two languages to be told apart the way any two languages are: by their grammar, with the quantum one simply refusing to accept a word like copy. That is not what happens, and what does happen is more useful.

The two languages share a single reader. Not two readers built alike — one reader, handed a different list of available steps. Which means copy is perfectly good Q42 text. The reader parses it quite happily and files it as a reference to some definition the file is expected to provide, exactly as it would file an invented word like frobnicate. At that stage the two are indistinguishable.

The consequence

Whether a procedure belongs to the quantum language is not something you can see by looking at it. Both files are perfectly well formed. What separates them is a lookup in a list of names, which happens afterwards.

That is not a technicality, because it is the mechanism behind the two things this paper claims most loudly. The file in §3.7 can be one file read by two readers only because neither reader throws out the other's text on sight. And the manifest checker of §5 can be a single shared checker only because the list of steps is something handed to it, rather than baked into the grammar. Were the boundary a matter of grammar, both would be impossible.

There is a consequence for the error message too. Since the refusal is not a grammatical one, it does not come for free — somebody had to write it. A name the reader cannot resolve gets one line; copy gets the reason.

How to read the transcripts, since this is the first one. The tool is invoked as

42q   <file>   <definition>   <input>

so 42q rejected bad "|0>" means: load the file rejected.42, find the definition called bad inside it, and run that on the input |0>. Later transcripts sometimes put an instruction first — 42q matrix gates h asks for the grid of the definition h in gates.42 rather than running it.

The two files below are one line each, and they exist only in order to be refused — hence the name of the definition in them. Notice that they are the same shape: a definition whose body is a single word the reader cannot resolve.

Same shape in, two very different messages out — and the difference is entirely in what somebody chose to say about a name the quantum language happens not to have.

And because the tool has to produce that text, the prose cannot quietly drift away from what the implementation does — which matters more here than anywhere else in the document, since §3.3's distinction (excluded for not reaching everywhere, not for cloning) is exactly the sort of claim that gets restated wrongly the third time somebody repeats it.

3.6Why this language and not another

The move of §3 is not available to reversible languages in general, and saying why locates what has actually been used. It also means finally making good on a phrase this document has now used three times without explaining: that the thirteen basic steps are the ones which say the manifest builders behave like arithmetic.

That is meant literally, not as a figure of speech, and the way to see it is to count the possible consignments.

Three kinds of pallet and five kinds of docket: then "a pallet or a docket" admits eight things, and "a pallet with a docket" admits fifteen. The bin builder is not like addition; on the counts it is addition, and the pairing builder is multiplication.

So every ordinary law of arithmetic turns into a statement about manifests — and each such statement, being a claim that two manifests admit exactly the same things, is a step that converts one into the other:

Seven laws, seven steps. Add "do nothing" and that is eight — which is exactly the number that survived §3.1, and that is not a coincidence:

What the section has been claiming

The steps that survive the change of arithmetic are precisely the ones that were laws of arithmetic in the first place. The five that go are the five that never were.

Which is the whole mechanism, and it is almost disappointingly simple once seen. A law of arithmetic does not mention what the numbers are. It is a statement about shape, and it stays true whatever the quantities happen to be. So change what the ledger holds, and every step witnessing a law goes on working, untouched, because it never said anything about the ledger to begin with. Nobody redesigns anything.

The other five were extras, added for a purpose of their own, and it shows in their shapes. Read them as equations and you get statements that are not laws: copying claims a = a × a, and merging the two bins claims a + a = a. Neither is true of ordinary arithmetic — though the second is perfectly true when the only quantities available are yes and no, which is §3.2 arriving from another direction.

And that is the price of admission

A language whose basic steps were chosen because they were useful has no such handle. Only a language whose steps were forced by a structure can have the structure swapped underneath it.

Inv is the closest relative: like this language it has no variables and describes plumbing rather than naming values, and it shares the same law about running backwards. But its basic steps are a toolkit rather than a set of laws — swap two things, regroup, duplicate, test whether two things are equal, test whether they are not. No bins, no splitting law, no token law anywhere in its core. And there is nothing there for a change of arithmetic to act on, because "these two are not equal" is not a law of anything. It is a useful test. You cannot ask what it means when the ledger holds quantities, because it never said anything about what the ledger held. The other languages fail the same test in their various ways.

So the entry ticket to this whole construction is having taken the basic steps from a structure rather than from a wishlist — a decision the classical paper makes for reasons with nothing whatever to do with quantum computing, and which turns out to be exactly what makes quantum computing reachable by rereading the text rather than by extending it.

3.7The classical part is exactly the rearrangements

Drop the five, and what is left is a well-defined sub-language, and a familiar one: everything built from the rearrangement steps by then, two bins and together is a pure shuffle. Read with yes/no those are exactly the reversible logic circuits; read with quantities they are exactly the shuffles. This is not a translation between two things — it is one text with two readings, and the file from §1 is the demonstration:

The classical run reports a set of results, because there the answer to a query is a shortlist. The quantum run reports a spread. The definitions are identical, and so is the arithmetic being done on the bins.

Section 4What is added

Removing five steps leaves a language of shuffles: enough for reversible logic, and no more. Two new basic steps are needed, and they are taken from an existing result.

A turn

Changes nothing about where anything is. It only rotates an arrow — by an eighth of a full circle. Sounds like doing nothing, and is the exact opposite: it is what decides whether two routes later reinforce or cancel.

A half-swap

Swapping the two bins of a lane is an ordinary step. This is half of it: do it twice and you have swapped. Do it once and you are somewhere in between — which takes a paragraph to say properly, below.

What a turn does

The turn is an eighth of a full circle — forty-five degrees — and it is the only turn the language has. So every other angle must be spelled as some number of eighths, and the directions an arrow can hold lie on a compass:

Two turns brings an arrow to straight up, which is the number written i. Four brings it to pointing backwards, which is −1. Eight brings it the whole way round, which is why the first of the three equations below reads "eight turns get you back where you started". The splitter of §0.2 only ever produced forward and backward arrows — the nought-turn and four-turn positions — and the angles in between are what the turn contributes.

What a half-swap is

Half of an operation is an ordinary idea, provided the operation is a motion. Turning a crate to face backwards is a half-turn; half of that is a quarter turn. Do the quarter turn twice and the crate faces backwards. Nothing strange has happened — and note that at the halfway point the crate is in a perfectly definite position. It simply is not one of the two positions you had been thinking about.

Swapping the two bins of a lane is the half-turn. The half-swap is its quarter turn.

Why is there room for a halfway point at all? Because a lane is not merely a bit. If the only thing it could hold were "bin 0" or "bin 1", there would be no position in between and no square root to take. But each bin carries an arrow, and the arrows supply a whole continuum of settings between the two definite ones. The half-swap lands on one of them.

Which is what the usual phrase is trying to say

"The consignment is in both bins at once" is a bad way of putting it. Nothing has been duplicated and nothing is in two places. The lane is at an intermediate setting, as definite as a crate at ninety degrees.

What makes it feel strange is §0.3. When you look, there is no intermediate outcome on offer — you must see bin 0 or bin 1, and which one is a matter of chance. The in-between setting is perfectly real and does real work; it is just never something you can read off directly.

And here is the demonstration worth running, because it catches the arrows earning their keep. The splitter and the half-swap both take a definite lane to a fifty-fifty spread. Look immediately after either one and you cannot tell them apart:

Two splitters return you to the start; two half-swaps leave you swapped. Same chances at every point, different operations throughout, and nothing but the arrow directions to tell them apart — which is as sharp a demonstration as this document can offer that the chances are not the whole story, and that the arrows are not a bookkeeping device.

The tool gives the arrows exactly, and they are worth seeing written down. After one splitter both halves read 0.707107, which is length 0.707 at nought turns. After one half-swap they read -0.5+0.5i and -0.5-0.5i, which are the same length 0.707 at three turns and five turns on the compass above — a quarter circle apart, and neither of them anywhere the splitter ever goes.

The existing result says that the plumbing plus these two steps, subject to three equations, is enough for all of quantum computing — and is moreover exactly right, neither too weak nor too strong, for three well-studied families of circuits. The three equations are: eight turns get you back where you started; two half-swaps make a swap; and one interleaving identity relating the two. All three are checkable by running them rather than assumed.

One remark before the catalogue. The turn acts on the token manifest — the one that carries no information at all — which is why a language with no numbers in it can express a rotation: a step from a token to a token cannot move anything, since there is nowhere to move it to, so the only thing it can possibly do is change an arrow. An operation on the thing that carries no information is, structurally, exactly what a number is. And it is the half-swap, not Hadamard, that is basic here: the splitter of §0.2 is the one piece of quantum equipment this document has described in detail, and it turns out to be derived — five ordinary steps and a turn, as the table records. Taking a square root of swapping rather than a basis change as the primitive is what makes the extension two steps rather than a whole gate catalogue.

Everything else is derived — here is the catalogue

The names below are the standard vocabulary of quantum computing, and the point of listing them is that not one of them is a feature of the language. Each is an ordinary definition in a file, twenty-six of them altogether, and you could delete the file without touching the language at all.

One idiom recurs and is worth reading once, because it demystifies half the table. "Two bins side by side" — one of the eight survivors of §3.1 — lets you say do one thing to the bin-0 half and something else to the bin-1 half. Write "leave bin 0 alone, and turn bin 1 four times" and you have written the gate everyone calls Z. So the phase gates are not new machinery at all; they are the ordinary bin plumbing with a turn in one arm.

Read the right-hand column downwards. Only two rows mention anything that was not already in the classical language: the turns, and the single half-swap inside Hadamard. Everything else is the plumbing of §2 and one combinator — controlled-by — applied over and over, to itself included.

That combinator needs no separate reverse, either. The reverse of "do m only if the flag is set" is automatically "do the reverse of m only if the flag is set". Physicists write that down as a law about controlled operations; here it simply falls out of the machinery, because reversal distributes over the pieces and the flag is untouched by it.

4.1What the two new steps actually buy

That two steps suffice is not this paper's theorem. What an implementation adds is the ability to watch the qualitative change happen.

Before the two new steps, every procedure is a shuffle, and a shuffle sends a definite arrangement to a definite arrangement. No spread can ever arise, whatever you write. Adding them changes that in a single move:

Two lanes, one splitter (§0.2) and one controlled flip. Read the output: the goods in the two lanes are either both in bin 0 or both in bin 1, with nothing in between. That is stronger than it looks. It does not mean "one of the two happened and we do not know which" — it means the two lanes no longer have separate states at all. There is no way to describe lane one on its own and lane two on its own such that putting the descriptions side by side gives you this. The lanes have become entangled: a single object with two ends, and whatever you eventually see at one end fixes what you would see at the other, however far apart you carry them.

Entanglement is the resource the interesting algorithms actually spend, and here it is checkable rather than asserted. Lay the four arrows out in a two-by-two square; the two lanes are separable exactly when a certain simple combination of the four comes to zero. Here it comes to a half. So entanglement, in this presentation, is a number failing to vanish — computed by the same reader that runs the classical shuffles.

That is the whole qualitative content of the change, in two definitions: controlled-not does nothing a shuffle cannot; Hadamard does; and the pair together produce something no combination of separate lanes can equal.

4.2Repetition is gone, and the host is the classical language

Losing "repeat" (§3.2) removes the language's only open-ended control construct, and the obvious question is what becomes of a procedure that has to do something a number of times. It divides into four cases, and only the last is a limit rather than a chore.

One — the count is fixed when you write it

Then just write it out. Three stations in a row rather than a loop that runs three times. A Fibonacci step on two small registers, composed with itself, walks the sequence and returns to its starting point after six steps — because a conserving step on a finite warehouse is a shuffle, and a shuffle repeated enough times always comes back round.

Two — the count is fixed by the size of the job

This is most real quantum algorithms. Grover's search runs roughly the square root of the number of possibilities; the standard frequency-analysis layout has a depth set by the number of lanes. Writing it out still suffices, because fixing the number of lanes fixes the count — and you have to fix the number of lanes anyway, since in this language the number of lanes is the manifest.

Grover's search, in six lines

Worth doing properly, because it is the algorithm this case is named after and because it shows the whole argument of §0.2 in one run. The problem: one of several possibilities is the one you want, and nothing distinguishes it except a sealed test that says yes to it and no to the rest. Classically you try them one at a time.

Found with certainty, in one turn of the crank. But run it in stages and you catch the interesting part:

Look at the middle line

The sealed test has run, and every outcome still has exactly the same chance as before — all four at a quarter. Look at that point and you learn nothing whatever. The only thing that changed in the whole step is one arrow's direction, which §0.3 says no measurement can see.

Then the last line. What the reflection does is convert an unmeasurable difference into a certain one — and it is built as h2 ; · ; h2, a splitter at each end, for exactly the reason §0.2 gives.

Now the point this case is about. Two lanes want one turn of the crank; three lanes want two — one turn on three lanes reaches 78.1%, and two reach 94.5%. So the number of turns is settled by the width of the register and by nothing else, which means it is known before the program runs and can simply be written out.

None of that is peculiar to this language. The standard model of quantum computation is a family of layouts: for each job size a layout, with the map from size to layout worked out by an ordinary classical program. No layout's shape is allowed to depend on the goods flowing through it. What the model does require is somewhere to write that classical program, and every quantum toolkit answers with a general-purpose host language — the big industrial one builds layouts from Python, another from Haskell. Here the host is the classical warehouse language itself, and §4.2.1 is that construction.

Three — the count is capped, but depends on the goods

Guard every step. Each pass becomes a station that acts only while a "still running" flag is set, so that passes after the job is done are stations that do nothing, and you write out the worst case. This needs nothing new — it is the controlled-by combinator that is already there:

Running past the end costs nothing at all, which is what makes a worst-case cap usable rather than merely correct. And note that the cap is itself usually a function of the number of lanes — so at any realistic size this case reduces to case two, and one host answers both.

Four — no cap at all

Here the language genuinely stops, and not by a design shortcut. Deciding whether to go round again means knowing something about the goods, which means looking, which means leaving the world where everything conserves — the same wall as §7.2, approached from the other side. The one existing language with real unbounded loops gets them exactly by paying that price: its loop condition is a measurement. And the authors of the other route judge the corresponding extension to "require answering fundamental open questions". The loss is over-determined.

The architectural answer is the one real hardware already uses. Shor's algorithm — the famous one — is a single quantum layout and one look at the end, with the number-theory step and the try-again-if-it-failed loop living in ordinary classical code wrapped around the outside. Unbounded repetition belongs to the classical driver, not to the layout.

4.2.1A layout family, generated

The quantum language cannot express a family of layouts. The classical language can — and the two are the same language up to what is in the ledger. Three ingredients, and only the third is new.

Layouts as goods

A layout is a tree: stations, "then", and "side by side". A tree is exactly what a self-referential manifest describes. So a layout is an ordinary consignment in the classical language, needing no new feature whatsoever.

The generator

A family is a computable map from a job size to such a tree, and the first paper proved the classical language expresses every listable behaviour — so every computable family is reachable. And crucially it is an ordinary self-referential definition, not a parameterised one, so the one-level-deep restriction never bites.

The bridge

A layout-as-goods is not yet a layout-as-procedure. Goods and procedures are different things. So something outside both languages has to hand one to the other — and that something is twenty lines, precisely because the two languages already share a parser and a manifest checker.

Every host has that last seam; the industrial toolkit's is the stage that consumes its layout objects. Here it is as narrow as it can possibly be, and the generated text is handed straight back to the same checkers the hand-written libraries use.

What gets generated

The standard frequency-analysis layout — the workhorse inside most quantum algorithms worth naming, including Shor's. Its recursive shape is: do a Hadamard on the first lane; then, controlled by that lane, apply a fan of turns to all the others; then do the whole thing again on the remaining lanes. The fan is the pretty part: the turns act on different lanes, so they all happen at once rather than one after another, and one control covers the lot.

Why the layout is approximate, and where you can see it

Recall that the only turn available is an eighth of a full rotation. The layout wants turns of a half, a quarter, an eighth, a sixteenth, and so on, halving each time — and the first three of those can be built from eighths while the fourth cannot. There is no exact sixteenth-turn in this language, because there is none in the standard gate set it is built from, which is universal only in the sense of getting arbitrarily close.

So the generator drops the turns finer than an eighth, which is what real hardware does anyway. And look at the second line of output above: the id sitting inside (s * (t * id)) is a station that does nothing. That is the approximation, showing up in the generated text at exactly the point where the available turns run out.

How good is it

At three lanes nothing is dropped, and the generated layout is the exact transform — verified against the textbook formula, agreeing to fifteen decimal places. At four lanes, where one turn is dropped, the worst-case faithfulness is 98.1%, and the tool confirms the result still conserves everything exactly.

And one thing no other host can do

The generator runs backwards

It is a program in a reversible language, and reversal is total, so it reverses like anything else. Feed the three-lane layout to the generator backwards and it returns the job size that produced it.

A plan-builder written in Python or Haskell has no reverse to offer. Here, a family generator inverts into a layout recogniser, for free.

Two things this does not supply. Being able to build the family is not the same as building it efficiently — that is a property of a particular generator, to be proved about it. And a layout tree is guaranteed to be well-formed, not to be a correctly-sized layout; saying that inside the language would need a much more powerful manifest system. So generated layouts are checked rather than correct by construction — which is cheap, because the checker is already shared.

4.3Control and the plumbing are the same thing

That "do this only if the flag is set" is derived here rather than being a basic step is not an economy; it is a theorem, and it belongs to somebody else. A recent result shows that if you take a bare set of stations and add seven rules describing how control behaves, what you generate is exactly the plumbing of §2 — the bins, the pairs, the distribution law, all of it. Their thesis, in their words, is that the plumbing "encapsulates controlled computation, and only controlled computation".

So control and plumbing are two views of one thing, and this paper and that one build from opposite ends: here the plumbing is basic and control is derived from the distribution law; there control is the added theory and the plumbing falls out.

A no-go theorem this language walks past

The same authors record an impossibility result: you cannot build a controlled version of an operation that is handed to you as a sealed box. If all you can do is feed things in and read things out, there is no arrangement of wires that turns it into "do this only if the flag is set".

The controlled-by combinator here is untroubled by that, for a reason that has nothing to do with physics. A parameter in this language stands for a piece of text, not for a device, and parameters are filled in before anything runs. So it never receives a sealed box — it receives a recipe, and expands. The one-level-deep restriction that the classical paper records as a limitation turns out to be precisely what keeps this construct on the right side of an impossibility result.

Section 5The manifest system is unchanged

Sharing a reader between two languages would be unremarkable. Sharing the manifest checker is the part that tests whether the change is really as local as claimed. It is.

Manifests are the classical manifests, unchanged: the four builders, self-reference allowed, worked out with no annotations. "A two-bin choice with arrows" is not a new kind of manifest, it is a name for one that already existed. So a register of several lanes is a nest of pairs, and the number two shows up because a lane is a choice between two things — the same reason it shows up classically.

The inference engine is not forked but instantiated: the table of basic steps is handed to it as an input, so the classical and quantum languages are one checker run with two different tables, and nothing in the machinery that solves the constraints knows the difference. Which means the checker reports things in the same form and with the same generality:

Read: given any procedure from something back to itself, produce a procedure from (a lane, together with that something) back to itself. The "something" is genuinely open, so controlled-controlled-not works out at three lanes with nothing written down anywhere. And the requirement that the controlled thing go from a manifest back to the same manifest is not imposed for quantum reasons at all — it falls out of the two branches of a bin choice having to meet up, which is equally true classically. The quantum reading gives it a physical gloss (a controlled station must return goods to the place it took them from), but the constraint was already there.

Where manifests and physics do diverge

Not every valid manifest is a valid lane layout. "One of three things" is a perfectly good manifest and a perfectly bad register — you cannot build it from two-way lanes. So a compiler targeting real hardware needs a side condition, and the right one is not "is the size a power of two" but a recursive width check. It is a dozen lines over machinery that already exists, and it does not bite: every non-parameterised definition in every library already satisfies it.

Section 6The rulebook, runnable

The existing result this paper builds on contributes a rulebook — rules for proving two layouts equivalent. Implementing it makes those rules something you execute rather than something you argue. Three commands do the work: one prints the grid a procedure denotes, one checks that each result reverses back, and one checks across a whole library that every procedure composed with its own reverse is the do-nothing procedure.

The two exceptions are not failures. They are the controlled-by combinator and one of its uses at unspecified size — things that take a parameter, and so have no single grid until the parameter is supplied. The tool says so rather than guessing.

Each of the three equations from §4, and each standard identity that follows from them, is a test in the suite rather than a claim in prose: two Hadamards cancel; Hadamard-then-flip-then-Hadamard is a phase flip; two quarter-turns make a half-turn; the reverse of a controlled step is the controlled reverse. They are checked by evaluating rather than by rewriting, which verifies the model rather than the calculus — the appropriate direction for an implementation paper, and the complement of the machine-checked development accompanying the other route, which checks the calculus.

The tool also prints arrows rather than only samples, which no physical device can. Deutsch's algorithm — the simplest thing that is genuinely faster on a quantum machine — is six lines, of which four are the four possible sealed questions being asked, all written in the classical part of the language (§3.7). So the quantum content sits visibly in the two Hadamards and nowhere else, rather than being hidden in a gate table. Run it and you get two outcomes with arrows; the first lane holds the same bin in both, so that bin is certain, and the answer is read off directly — and you can see why, rather than inferring it from a histogram, which is all a real device offers.

Section 7Looking

7.1Looking at the end costs nothing

Looking is not a step, and §0.3 says why: it cannot be undone, and it is not even predictable, since which outcome you see is a matter of chance weighted by the squares of the arrow lengths. Every other operation in a quantum machine is a reversible rearrangement. This one is not, and it is the only one.

So the language does not contain it. Looking is an operation on the finished output: take the spread the procedure produced, square the arrow lengths to get the chances, and draw. No construct was added, reversal is untouched, the manifest system is untouched, and conservation-by-construction survives entirely intact. It is one rule and a random number.

That covers every algorithm whose last step is a single look — Deutsch, Bernstein–Vazirani, Grover, and the quantum part of Shor.

7.2Looking partway through is refused, and costs nothing

Looking within a procedure is absent by decision. A "look here" step would be one whose reverse has no answer, and every property in §3 would become conditional on where you had put your looks.

The refusal is affordable because of a theorem, and it is a genuinely surprising one:

Deferred looking

Any procedure that looks partway through and then acts differently depending on what it saw can be rewritten so that all the looking happens at the very end — by replacing "act on what I saw" with "act under quantum control", which is the controlled-by combinator that is already there.

The canonical example is teleportation — the protocol usually offered as the reason a language needs mid-procedure looking. Here it runs as a single conserving procedure on three lanes, verified by the tool. The two classical bits are never extracted at all: the four possible outcomes stay in the spread, and the corrections at the far end are applied under control. The paper checks it by preparing a state carrying a genuine arrow direction, teleporting it, and undoing the preparation at the other end — returning to the starting point with certainty is the test.

The contrast with the other route is exact. There, looking is obtained by adding yet another layer, built on an operation whose own authors write is "dangerous, as it does not enforce that it is only applied to total maps". Which is the honest general statement:

The general point

Adding looking to a conserving language is not an addition to it. It is leaving the conserving world. This language stays, and pays by keeping looking outside.

7.3A stage that undoes the deferral

Deferring is free in a simulation and not free on hardware. A lane whose look has been postponed goes on consuming the machine's limited coherence time and cannot be reused for anything else. So a compiler emitting these procedures to a real device would want to run the theorem of §7.2 backwards: spot a controlled station whose control lane is never touched again, and emit look-and-branch where the text says quantum control.

That is a rewrite over something the language already expresses, not a feature it lacks. To the paper's knowledge no previous language requires such a stage — because none of them is structurally obliged to defer in the first place. It is stated as a consequence of the design rather than as an implemented optimisation, and it is the clearest piece of future work the paper has.

Section 8Neighbours, limits, and what this is not

Related work

The background observation — that the world of yes/no grids and the world of quantum operations satisfy the same structural laws — is long established, and the first world has long been used as a toy model for quantum protocols on that basis. This paper differs from the toy-model tradition in direction: the question is not what quantum-like behaviour the classical world exhibits, but what a language written for the classical world loses when the world is replaced.

Among implemented systems, Quipper is the closest in ambition — an embedded layout-generating toolchain aimed at estimating resources, and, like the industrial toolkit, a layout generator hosted in a general-purpose classical language, which is what §4.2.1 does with the classical twin. Silq is closest in spirit: its headline feature is working out where to insert the undoing of temporary work, which here is three characters, because reversal is part of the language and is exact rather than inferred. Neither shares an implementation with a classical reversible language, which is this paper's specific claim.

On the cost of simulating

A station acting on n lanes is a grid with two-to-the-n rows, so running these procedures on an ordinary computer costs time and memory that double with every added lane. That does not distinguish this from any other classical simulator, the industrial ones included; a quantum language is not in competition with quantum hardware. One genuine nuance: a known result says that layouts built only from a certain restricted family of stations can be simulated cheaply despite looking thoroughly quantum, entanglement and all. It is the eighth-turn that takes a layout beyond that family — and this language has it.

Limitations

  • The layouts are now emitted, in the standard interchange format, and every definition in every library comes out and is checked against what the language itself says it means. What is emitted is unoptimised, though: correctness came first, and tidying it is a separable job.
  • No noise model, no error correction, no gate fidelities. Looking is at the end only, by the decision of §7.2.
  • Of the four repetition regimes of §4.2, only the last is a limitation of the language. The first three are all discharged by writing the stations out, and the writing-out is done by the host of §4.2.1 rather than by hand. The last cannot be lifted, since it requires leaving the conserving world.
  • The host has the two caveats §4.2.1 records: efficiency is proved of a particular generator rather than supplied by the manifest system, and generated layouts are checked rather than correct by construction.

How to read the overlap

A measurement, not a commitment

The sharing this document keeps pointing at — one reader, one manifest checker, one reversal routine — is a finding, not a design goal. The classical language was built for translating between human languages (§0.4). Quantum computing was not in view, and nothing about it was chosen to make any of this work.

Which is exactly what makes the overlap worth reporting. The parts that coincide do so because the mathematics makes them coincide, and a project that had set out to keep the two aligned could claim far less from the same measurements.

It follows that nothing obliges the two to stay aligned, and the emitter is the natural place to stop trying: the classical language has no device to target, so an emitter has nothing to share with it. Whatever else a quantum language turns out to need — noise, resource estimation, lowering for particular hardware — can be built without any of it counting as a loss. The interesting number was never how much is shared. It is which parts turned out to be shareable, and why.

What this is not

It is not a claim that changing the arithmetic is better than adding machinery; the two routes land in different worlds and answer to different questions. It is not a new proof that this is enough for quantum computing — that result belongs to somebody else. And it is not an argument that quantum programming ought to be done this way. It is the observation that a great deal of what a quantum language needs is already present in a classical reversible one, and that the leftover is small enough to write down exactly.

Section 9Conclusion

Rereading a classical reversible language with quantities instead of yes/no costs five constructs and buys two, and the five go under two independent rules — "adding must not double" for the two choice constructs, conservation for the other three. Everything else, including the reversal operator, the reader and the entire manifest system, is shared code. And the word "possible" that the classical language pays for declining uniqueness collapses into "is" the moment the ledger holds quantities.

Two things are worth remembering.

Why copying is really excluded

Not by the famous no-cloning rule — which it does not violate, only ever duplicating definite arrangements — but by the plainer failure of not being able to reach everywhere. The distinction is exactly the sort of thing an implementation forces you to get right, and prose alone lets you get wrong.

What the twin says for it

What the quantum language cannot express, its classical sibling expresses on its behalf. The loop that "repeat" used to provide comes back as a generator, written in the same syntax, checked by the same checker — and inverting for free.

Artifact

One repository implements both languages, with no external dependencies. The quantum reader and its table of basic steps come to roughly three hundred lines; everything else is shared with the classical language.
