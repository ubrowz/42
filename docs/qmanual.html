For a reader with a computer science background who has read the 42 manual: its Part I, which writes definitions as clauses (Q42's clauses follow stricter rules, §7.3), and its Part II, the point-free core that 42 and Q42 share. No prior knowledge of quantum mechanics is assumed; the physics is developed here from scratch, but only as far as Q42 actually needs it, and the parts Q42 does not model are named as such.

What Q42 is. 42 computes with sets: a program relates inputs to outputs, and running it backwards gives the set of everything an answer could have come from. Q42 is the same language computing with complex numbers: each input–output pairing carries a number, those numbers can cancel, and that makes it a language for quantum computers. §1 says why such a language has to be reversible, §2 gives the physics, and §3 and §4 show how little of 42 had to change.

#1. Why reversibility is forced

Before the physics, the engineering reason a computer scientist should care.

#Erasing a bit costs energy

Landauer's principle (1961): erasing one bit of information in a computer at temperature T must turn at least kT ln 2 joules of energy into heat. T is the absolute temperature, in kelvin, and k is Boltzmann's constant, a fixed number from physics that converts temperature into energy. At room temperature the product is about 2.9 × 10⁻²¹ joules, negligible per bit today, and a floor that no cleverness removes.

The point is why there is a floor. Information that is destroyed does not vanish: it is pushed out into the computer's surroundings as disorder, which physicists call entropy, and producing entropy costs heat. A computation that never destroys information has no such floor. Bennett showed in 1973 that any computation can be rearranged into one that destroys nothing, by keeping a record of whatever an ordinary program would have thrown away: the same trick as mul keeping its multiplier in the 42 manual's §9.3. That is the origin of reversible computing as a field, and of 42.

#Quantum mechanics has no choice at all

A quantum computer stores information in qubits, the quantum counterpart of bits, which §2.1 introduces. Like a classical computer it works by applying a sequence of elementary operations to them, and these are called gates, as in a classical logic circuit; a drawing of the sequence is a circuit, in which each qubit is a horizontal line, a wire, and each gate a box on the wires it acts on.

For a quantum computer, reversibility stops being an efficiency argument and becomes a constraint. Physics says that an isolated quantum system changes in a way described by a particular kind of matrix, a unitary one (§2.3 defines it), and every unitary matrix has an inverse. So every quantum gate can be undone. There is no such thing as an irreversible quantum gate, in the way that there is no such thing as a triangle with four sides. It is not a design decision anyone made.

So the familiar irreversible operations are simply unavailable:

c = a AND b

destroys information, since knowing c = 0 tells you almost nothing about a and b, so there is no two-input AND gate. What exists instead is the Toffoli gate, which keeps its two inputs and writes the answer into a third wire, by exclusive-or (XOR, addition mod 2):

(a, b, c)  |->  (a, b, c XOR (a AND b))

Set c = 0 and the third component becomes a AND b, with a and b still there. Nothing is lost, so the gate can be undone: in fact it undoes itself, since applying it twice XORs the same value in twice. This is the padding trick of the 42 manual's §9.3 again; the same pressure produces the same answer in both places.

#Uncomputation

Padding has a cost: the kept-around values pile up. If you compute an intermediate x, use it, and no longer want it, you cannot drop it, since that would be erasing information. You must uncompute it by running its producer backwards.

The pattern is P ; Q ; P!: do the work, use it, undo the work. In Q42 that is an ordinary term, because ! is part of the language, and because every Q42 term is invertible P! really is P's inverse. Some quantum languages decide for you where the undo goes: Silq (Bichsel et al., 2020) infers it, so that the programmer never has to. Q42 does not infer: you decide, and then writing it costs three characters. What the language gives you is that what you wrote is exact.

Enough to read Q42 programs, and no more. Everything here is finite-dimensional linear algebra over the complex numbers; nothing requires an understanding of physics.

Complex numbers, in one paragraph. A complex number is c = a + bi, with a and b real and i² = −1. Its magnitude |c| is its distance from 0 in the plane, √(a² + b²), so |c|² = a² + b². Its conjugate is a − bi, written conj(c) or c*; and c × conj(c) = |c|². A complex number of magnitude 1 lies on the unit circle and can be written e^{iθ} = cos θ + i sin θ, the point at angle θ (in radians) from the positive real axis. Multiplying by such a number rotates by θ and changes no magnitude; it is called a phase. Four of them recur below: 1 (angle 0), i (a quarter turn, π/2), −1 (a half turn, π), and e^{iπ/4} = (1 + i)/√2, an eighth of a turn.

#2.1 A state is a vector of amplitudes

A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit is a pair of complex numbers, one attached to each of those two possibilities.

Physicists write this with kets. |x> means "the basis state labelled x", so |0> and |1> are a qubit's two basis states, the quantum counterparts of the bit values 0 and 1. A qubit's state in general is written:

|psi>  =  c0 |0>  +  c1 |1>          c0, c1 in C

Read it as: the state called psi consists of the basis state 0 with weight c0, plus the basis state 1 with weight c1, where c0 and c1 are complex numbers (C is the complex numbers). psi, the Greek letter ψ, is only the customary name for "some state", the way x is for "some number"; nothing depends on it. The weights c0 and c1 are the amplitudes, and §2.2 says what they mean. When c1 is 0 the state is just |0>, and when c0 is 0 it is just |1>. When both are nonzero, the qubit is in both basis states at once, in proportions the amplitudes give: a superposition, which §2.4 takes up.

As a computer scientist would store it, the state is the array [c0, c1], a vector of length two. That is all a ket is: |0> is [1, 0], |1> is [0, 1], and c0 |0> + c1 |1> is [c0, c1].

For n qubits there are 2ⁿ basis states, |000…> through |111…>, one for each n-bit string, and a state is a vector of 2ⁿ amplitudes, one per string. That exponential is the whole reason simulating quantum computers is expensive, and the whole reason building them might be worth it.

#2.2 Amplitudes are not probabilities

You cannot read the amplitudes off a real qubit. What you can do is measure it: a measurement returns a classical bit string, one of the basis states, at random, and afterwards the qubits are left in exactly the basis state that was returned, so measuring again gives the same answer. The probability of each outcome comes from its amplitude: you see outcome x with probability |cₓ|², the squared magnitude. This is the Born rule, and it is where the physics enters; the rest is linear algebra.

Two consequences a computer scientist should internalise:

  • Probabilities must sum to 1, so Σ |cᵢ|² = 1: the squared magnitudes of a state's amplitudes add up to one. In other words a state is a vector of length 1, a unit vector.
  • Amplitudes can be negative, or complex. Probabilities cannot. This is the entire difference between a quantum computer and a randomised classical one, and everything interesting comes from it.

#2.3 A gate is a unitary matrix

A gate on n qubits is a 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ matrix of complex numbers, and applying it to a state is ordinary matrix–vector multiplication. For one qubit, name the gate's four entries by row and column, u00 to u11, and take the state [c0, c1] of §2.1:

  [ u00  u01 ]     [ c0 ]     [ u00 × c0  +  u01 × c1 ]     new amplitude of |0>
  [ u10  u11 ]  x  [ c1 ]  =  [ u10 × c0  +  u11 × c1 ]     new amplitude of |1>

Each new amplitude is one row of the matrix, multiplied entry by entry with the state and summed. Applied to |0>, which is [1, 0], the result is [u00, u10], the matrix's first column; applied to |1> it is the second column. So column k of the matrix is what the basis state |k> turns into, and a gate is fully described by what it does to each basis state.

An example with numbers, using a matrix chosen because its arithmetic comes out round, applied to the state 0.6|0> + 0.8|1>:

  [ 0.6   0.8 ]     [ 0.6 ]     [ 0.6 × 0.6  +  0.8 × 0.8 ]     [ 0.36 + 0.64 ]     [ 1 ]
  [ 0.8  -0.6 ]  x  [ 0.8 ]  =  [ 0.8 × 0.6  -  0.6 × 0.8 ]  =  [ 0.48 - 0.48 ]  =  [ 0 ]

The result is |0> exactly: in the second row the two products cancel, a first glimpse of §2.5. Physicists write the result of applying gate U to state |ψ> as U|ψ>.

The simplest example is NOT, written X and in Q42 called x. It swaps the two amplitudes, so |0> becomes |1> and |1> becomes |0>:

$ 42q matrix gates x
x : qubit <-> qubit
     |0>  [ 0  1 ]
     |1>  [ 1  0 ]

Read the column for |0> (the first): its 1 is in the row for |1>. (42q matrix gates x prints the matrix of the definition x in the Q42 library file q42/gates.42; §7.4 lists the commands.)

Not every matrix is allowed. A gate must satisfy

U† U  =  U U†  =  I

where I is the identity matrix, 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere, and U† ("U dagger") is the adjoint of U: swap rows and columns (transpose) and replace every entry by its conjugate. Such a matrix is called unitary.

Two things follow, and they are why the condition is the one physics imposes:

  • U† is U's inverse. Reversibility, for free, for every gate.
  • U preserves length. Since states are unit vectors and probabilities are squared magnitudes, a map that changed length would produce probabilities not summing to 1. Unitarity is exactly the condition "still a valid state afterwards".

Q42 spells U† as U!, which is the same ! that 42 uses for running a program backwards. It is not an analogy: in 42 the operation is the converse of a relation, in Q42 the adjoint of a matrix, and they are the same structural operation in two different settings (§4).

Q42 never checks unitarity, because it never has to: every primitive is unitary and every way of combining them preserves unitarity (§6). You can audit the claim anyway. For a definition t, 42q unitary builds the matrix of t ; t!, t followed by its own adjoint, which is U† U from the equation above, and checks that it is the identity: that every basis state comes back exactly as it went in. Run on a whole file, it does this for every definition in it:

$ 42q unitary gates
  [ok ] h ; h! = id on 2 dimension(s)
  [ok ] ccx ; ccx! = id on 8 dimension(s)
  ...
-- 24/26 unitary, 2 with no single matrix

gates.42 is the library of standard gates that §6 introduces; what each one does does not matter here, only that the check passes. Each [ok ] line names one definition and the size of the space it was checked on: 2ⁿ dimensions for a gate on n qubits, so 2 for a gate on one qubit and 8 for one on three. The last line counts them. The two "with no single matrix" are definitions that take another gate as a parameter, such as "the controlled version of a gate m" (§6.4). Until m is given there is no single matrix to check, so they are listed but not counted as failures.

#2.4 Superposition

A state with more than one nonzero amplitude is in superposition. The gate that makes one is the Hadamard gate, written H and in Q42 called h. Its matrix is

H  =  1/√2 × [ 1   1 ]          H|0>  =  ( |0> + |1> ) / √2
             [ 1  -1 ]          H|1>  =  ( |0> - |1> ) / √2

so it sends |0> to an equal superposition of both basis states, and |1> to the same with a minus sign on the second. Applied to |0>:

$ 42q gates h "|0>"
h |0>
  = 0.707107|0>  +  0.707107|1>

(42q gates h "|0>" runs h from q42/gates.42 on the state |0> and prints the resulting state.) 0.707107 is 1/√2, so both outcomes have probability (1/√2)² = 1/2: measure this qubit and you get 0 or 1, each half the time.

It is tempting, and wrong, to read this as "the qubit is secretly 0 or 1 and we don't know which". The next section is why.

#2.5 Interference — the one that matters

Apply h a second time. Classically, randomising a coin twice leaves it random. Here (hh is defined in gates.42 as h ; h):

$ 42q gates hh "|0>"
hh |0>
  = |0>

The randomness is gone. Follow the arithmetic. H|0> is 0.7071|0> + 0.7071|1>; now apply H to each part and collect the results:

    from |0> -> |0> :  +0.7071 x +0.7071  =  +0.5
    from |1> -> |0> :  +0.7071 x +0.7071  =  +0.5      sum: +1.0
    from |0> -> |1> :  +0.7071 x +0.7071  =  +0.5
    from |1> -> |1> :  +0.7071 x -0.7071  =  -0.5      sum:  0.0

Each line follows one route: the amplitude the first H gave a basis state, times the amplitude the second H sends it on with (the -0.7071 is the H|1> minus sign). Two routes lead to |1> and they arrive with opposite signs, so they cancel. This is destructive interference, and it has no classical counterpart: probabilities are non-negative and adding more routes to an outcome can only make it likelier. Amplitudes are not so constrained.

Every quantum algorithm that beats its classical rival does it this way, by arranging for the wrong answers' amplitudes to cancel and the right answer's to reinforce. The two famous ones are both this, elaborately: Grover's algorithm, which searches N unsorted items in about √N steps instead of N, and Shor's algorithm, which factors integers in polynomial time, where no classical algorithm is known to.

This is what 42 cannot express. Over the booleans the two routes to |1> would be 1 ∨ 1 = 1, still possible, and there is no −1 to cancel with. 42 tells you which outcomes are reachable; it cannot tell you that two ways of reaching one annihilate.

#2.6 Entanglement

Two qubits have four basis states, |00>, |01>, |10> and |11>, so a state of the pair is four amplitudes, c00, c01, c10 and c11. Sometimes those four come from two separate qubits, "this one is in state ψ and that one in state φ" (φ, phi, is just a second name for a state). Sometimes they do not, and then the qubits are entangled: the pair has a state, but neither qubit has one of its own.

The standard example is bell, two gates in a row: the Hadamard of §2.4 on the first qubit, then a controlled-NOT, which flips the second qubit when the first is 1 and leaves it alone when the first is 0. Started on |00>:

$ 42q gates bell "|00>"
bell |00>
  = 0.707107|00>  +  0.707107|11>

The arithmetic, step by step. Write a two-qubit state as a column of its four amplitudes, in the order |00>, |01>, |10>, |11>, and a gate as the 4×4 matrix that acts on it: the column for each input holds the amplitudes that input produces. With r = 1/√2 ≈ 0.7071, the starting state |00> is the column (1, 0, 0, 0).

The Hadamard on the first qubit sends |0x> to r|0x> + r|1x> and |1x> to r|0x> − r|1x>, whatever x is. Multiplying:

   H on the first qubit         |00>         after step 1

  [ r   0   r   0 ]            [ 1 ]          [ r ]    |00>
  [ 0   r   0   r ]     x      [ 0 ]    =     [ 0 ]    |01>
  [ r   0  -r   0 ]            [ 0 ]          [ r ]    |10>
  [ 0   r   0  -r ]            [ 0 ]          [ 0 ]    |11>

So after step 1 the state is r|00> + r|10>: the first qubit is in superposition and the second is still plainly 0. That much is a product, as §2.4's h would lead you to expect.

The controlled-NOT exchanges |10> and |11> and leaves |00> and |01> where they are. As a matrix that is a permutation:

      CNOT                   after step 1      after step 2

  [ 1   0   0   0 ]            [ r ]            [ r ]    |00>
  [ 0   1   0   0 ]     x      [ 0 ]    =       [ 0 ]    |01>
  [ 0   0   0   1 ]            [ r ]            [ 0 ]    |10>
  [ 0   0   1   0 ]            [ 0 ]            [ r ]    |11>

The amplitude that was on |10> has moved to |11>, and the result is r|00> + r|11>, which is what Q42 printed.

Why that cannot be two separate qubits. Suppose it were: the first qubit in a0|0> + a1|1>, the second in b0|0> + b1|1>. Then each two-qubit amplitude is a product of one from each, because the first qubit has to be in its part and the second in its:

c00 = a0 b0      c01 = a0 b1      c10 = a1 b0      c11 = a1 b1

The Bell state needs c01 = a0 b1 = 0, so a0 = 0 or b1 = 0. But a0 = 0 would make c00 = 0, and b1 = 0 would make c11 = 0, and both of those are r. No choice of the four numbers works, so the state is not a product.

The same argument gives a test that works on any two-qubit state. For a product, c00 c11 = a0 b0 a1 b1 = c01 c10, so

c00 c11  -  c01 c10  =  0

always, and conversely when this is zero the state does factor. (It is the determinant of the amplitudes arranged as a 2×2 grid, rows for the first qubit and columns for the second.) For the Bell state:

c00 c11 - c01 c10  =  0.7071 x 0.7071 - 0 x 0  =  0.5

which is not zero, so it does not factor.

What it means. Measure the pair and you get 00 or 11, each half the time, and never 01 or 10 (§7.6 shows 42q sample doing this). Each qubit on its own is a fair coin; together they always agree. So far that is no stranger than two sealed envelopes holding copies of one randomly chosen card. The strangeness appears when, before measuring, each side applies its own one-qubit gate that mixes |0> and |1> by some angle (a rotation of the qubit, of which the Hadamard is one). How often the two answers then agree depends on the angle between the two rotations, and for some pairs of angles the agreement is stronger than any envelopes, prepared in any way, could produce. That is Bell's theorem, which the state is named after. It does not let either side send a message to the other, but it is a resource quantum algorithms use, and §8.5's teleportation is built on exactly this state.

Entanglement is why Q42 needs one piece of machinery 42 did not (§7.5): a state of a compound system is not in general a pair of states.

#2.7 What Q42 does not model

Stated plainly, because a manual that quietly omits these would be misleading.

  • Measurement, except at the end. Q42 computes the unitary. You may measure the result (§7.6), which is the Born rule applied to a finished state, but nothing collapses mid-program, so there is no measuring, branching on the outcome, and carrying on. §9.2 is precise about the difference.
  • Normalisation. Q42 does not enforce Σ|cᵢ|² = 1. It does not need to: a ket you type in is a unit vector, and unitary maps preserve length, so a well-formed program cannot leave that set.
  • Noise, decoherence, error correction. Real qubits are disturbed by their surroundings and gradually lose their state, which is called decoherence; real gates are slightly inaccurate, which is noise; and error correction spreads each logical qubit over many physical ones so that such errors can be detected and repaired. None of this is modelled: Q42's qubits and gates are perfect.
  • Global phase. Multiplying an entire state by a phase, a complex number

e^{iθ} of magnitude 1, changes no squared magnitude and so no measurement outcome: the two states are physically indistinguishable. Q42 tracks such a factor faithfully anyway, which is why some gate identities hold only "up to phase", that is, only up to such a factor (§6.3).

With the physics in hand, here is the whole difference between 42 and Q42, made concrete. The way to see it is to draw a 42 program as a grid and then change what sits in the cells: marks in 42, the amplitudes of §2 in Q42.

#3.1 A program is a grid

Take a shape with finitely many values, say the two-value shape 1 + 1, whose values are L () and R () (the 42 manual, §3.1). A program from that shape to itself either can or cannot turn each input into each output, so you can write the whole program down as a grid: one row per input, one column per output, and a mark where the program can get from one to the other.

Here is not, which is the primitive swapsum: it moves a value from one label to the other (the 42 manual's §14 lists the primitives used in this section):

        to  L ()  R ()
  from
  L ()        .     *
  R ()        *     .

Read a row as "what this input can produce". L () produces only R (), which is what negation does.

A program need not have exactly one mark per row. inl!, the primitive inl run backwards, removes an L label and rejects anything else, so its row for R () is empty. That is 42's {}, drawn:

        to    ()
  from
  L ()        *
  R ()        .

And join! has two marks in one row. join removes a label and forgets which one it was; run backwards, it cannot know which to put back, so it offers both. That is a set of two answers, drawn:

        to  L ()  R ()
  from
    ()        *     *

Nothing new has been said yet. This is the same "a program returns a set" from the 42 manual's §6, written as a picture instead of as a list.

#3.2 Running backwards is reading the other way

Now the useful part. To run a program backwards you ask, of an output, which inputs could have produced it, which is reading the grid down a column instead of along a row.

So ! is the grid, flipped about its diagonal. Compare join! above with join:

                                        to    ()
        to  L ()  R ()           from
  from                    !  =   L ()        *
    ()        *     *            R ()        *

Same marks, rows and columns exchanged. This is why 42 never needed a second algorithm for the backward direction, and why f!! is f: flipping twice puts everything back.

#3.3 Composing is combining grids

f ; g means "do f, then g". So f ; g can get from x to z exactly when there is some middle value y with f reaching y and g reaching z from it. In grid terms: to fill in cell (x, z), walk along row x of f and down column z of g, and look for a position where both have a mark.

That rule, for each middle value take AND, and across all middle values take OR, is what mathematicians call matrix multiplication, with and in place of × and or in place of +. So 42's grids are matrices whose entries are 0 and 1, and ; is matrix multiplication over those. That is all that "42 is matrices over the booleans" means.

#3.4 Q42 puts numbers in the cells

Q42 changes one thing: a cell holds a complex number instead of a mark: the amplitude of §2.2.

Here is h, the Hadamard gate, whose grid Q42 will print for you:

$ 42q matrix gates h
h : qubit <-> qubit
     |0>  [  0.707107   0.707107 ]
     |1>  [  0.707107  -0.707107 ]

Same 2×2 grid as not, but with numbers, and one of them is negative. That is the point.

One wrinkle before going on. The grids in §3.1 put inputs in rows, which reads naturally as "from, to". 42q matrix prints them the other way up: its rows are outputs and its columns are inputs. That is the universal convention in physics, because a gate is written as acting on a column of amplitudes, U|ψ>. The two pictures hold the same information, one being the other transposed, and the difference does not show on h, whose grid happens to be symmetric. It does show on x ; s: x is NOT, and s is a gate §6.2 defines that leaves |0> alone and multiplies |1> by i. So x ; s takes |0> to |1> and then to i|1>, and its matrix has its lone i in row |1>, column |0>.

Composition works exactly as in §3.3, with the OR and AND becoming ordinary + and ×, since that is what they were standing in for. So compose h with itself and fill in the |1> cell of the first row: walk along h's row for |0> and down h's column for |1>, multiplying and adding.

    via |0> :  +0.7071 x +0.7071  =  +0.5000
    via |1> :  +0.7071 x -0.7071  =  -0.5000
    total   :                        0.0000

The two routes cancel: this is §2.5's interference, now seen as arithmetic on grids. With marks it could not have happened: * or * is *, and more ways of reaching an answer only ever makes it more reachable. With numbers, one route can undo another.

Two names for the record, since the literature uses them. A system with an addition and a multiplication but no need for subtraction is a semiring; {0,1} with or/and is one, and ℂ is another, so "Q42 is 42 over a different semiring" is a one-line way to say all of the above. And flipping-and-conjugating a matrix is its adjoint M†, from §2.3. So ! denotes the converse of a relation in 42 and the adjoint of a matrix in Q42, which §4.1 shows is the same operation twice.

#4. Why 42 already fitted

  • 4.1 inv and † are the same operation
  • 4.2 42's primitives were the right ones already

Q42 differs from 42 in very little, and the reason is structural rather than accidental.

#4.1 inv and † are the same operation

Mathematicians call a collection of things that can be composed, with an identity for composition, a category: programs with ; and id are one, and so are matrices with multiplication and the identity matrix. Both settings here are dagger categories, categories with an extra operation † that turns each thing around and satisfies

(f ; g)†  =  g† ; f†                 inversion reverses composites
f††       =  f                       it is an involution
id†       =  id                      it fixes identities

Relations satisfy these with † = converse. Complex matrices satisfy them with † = conjugate transpose. This is the founding observation of categorical quantum mechanics (Abramsky & Coecke, 2004), and it means rel42/core.py's dagger function is already the right function; only the primitive table it consults has to change.

The two laws, side by side:

42 :   x in P(y)      <=>   y in inv(P)(x)
Q42:   <x| P |y>       =    conj( <y| P! |x> )

inv(P) is 42's P!. <x| P |y> is the matrix entry of P in row x, column y: the amplitude with which P takes |y> to |x>. So the second line says that the amplitude for P to go from y to x is the conjugate of the amplitude for P! to go back. It is the same law as the first, with membership replaced by amplitude equality. You can check it; 42q law runs a gate on a state and compares each such pair of entries:

$ 42q law gates h "|0>"
h |0> has 2 component(s)
  [ok ] |0>: <y|P|x> = 0.707107, <x|P!|y> = 0.707107
  [ok ] |1>: <y|P|x> = 0.707107, <x|P!|y> = 0.707107
law holds

#4.2 42's primitives were the right ones already

42's types obey the laws of arithmetic: a + b has as many values as b + a, 1 × a as many as a, (a + b) × c as many as a×c + b×c, and so on. 42's primitives are exactly the conversions between the two sides of these laws: swapsum converts a + b into b + a, unitprod converts 1 × a into a, dist converts (a + b) × c into a×c + b×c. Each is a bijection, an isomorphism in the mathematician's word. Types with these conversions form what is called a rig groupoid ("rig" because the laws are those of a ring without subtraction, "groupoid" because every conversion can be undone). The reversible programming language Π of James & Sabry (2012) is built from exactly these conversions; 42 arrived at the same set independently.

This matters because of a result of Carette, Heunen, Kaarsgaard & Sabry (POPL 2024): a rig groupoid extended with two maps and three equations is computationally universal for quantum computing, that is, it can approximate any quantum computation as closely as you like. Those two maps are §6. Q42 is that construction, given 42's syntax: the point-free core, and clauses that compile to it (§7.3).

So the whole of the difference is: the semiring changes, the primitives that are not unitary go, and two unitary ones arrive. The evaluator, the type system and dagger are 42's, unchanged.

#5. What Q42 drops

  • 5.1 | and ^ need 1 + 1 = 1
  • 5.2 copy is not the no-cloning theorem
  • 5.3 Where the restriction actually bites
  • 5.4 What it means for clauses

Five primitives and two combining forms. Each is dropped for a reason you can check, not by taste, and §5.4 says what that changes for definitions written as clauses.

#5.1 | and ^ need 1 + 1 = 1

This one is exact. Over the booleans x ∨ x = x, so adding is idempotent. Over ℂ, 1 + 1 = 2.

f | g means "either", which as a matrix is ⟦f⟧ + ⟦g⟧, where ⟦f⟧ is the matrix of f. Over the booleans f | f is f; over ℂ it would be 2f, which is not the same relation and not even unitary. And f^ collects everything reachable by repeating f any number of times, stopping when nothing new appears (the 42 manual's §13); over ℂ, repeating adds amplitudes that never settle.

Write def bad = id | id in a Q42 file and it is refused before it runs, with the reason: "over C it would denote the sum of two matrices, so f | f would be 2f. Superposition comes from v, not from choice." (v is one of the two primitives Q42 adds; section 6.)

There is a trap worth naming. | looks like where superposition should come from, since over ℂ it is literally adding two vectors. It is not. Superposition comes from applying a gate (v or h) to a definite state. Keeping | would buy nothing but the ability to write non-unitary sums.

#5.2 copy is not the no-cloning theorem

copy : a ↔ a × a is dropped, and the reason is more interesting than "quantum states can't be copied".

Write |ψ> ⊗ |φ> for the two-qubit state in which the first qubit is in state ψ and the second in state φ: the product state of §2.6, whose amplitudes are products of the two. The no-cloning theorem says there is no unitary U with U(|ψ> ⊗ |0>) = |ψ> ⊗ |ψ> for every ψ: no gate copies an unknown state onto a blank qubit. 42's copy does not attempt that. It maps each basis state |x> to |x,x>, and like every gate it acts on a superposition by acting on each basis state and adding the results with the same amplitudes. So it sends |0>+|1> to |00>+|11>, which is the Bell state of §2.6, not (|0>+|1>)⊗(|0>+|1>). That is a perfectly legitimate quantum operation. In the categorical literature such a copying map is called a classical structure, and Coecke and Pavlović showed that each one amounts to choosing a measurement basis: the set of states a measurement tells apart, of which |0> and |1> is the standard one.

So why is it out? Because it is not square. It maps a space of dimension n into one of dimension n², one qubit's 2 amplitudes into two qubits' 4, and a unitary must map a space to itself. A length-preserving map into a larger space is called an isometry; every unitary is an isometry, but not conversely.

q42/rejected.42 contains nothing but def bad = copy, so that you can see the refusal for yourself:

$ 42q rejected bad "|0>"
error: `bad` uses `copy`, a 42 primitive that Q42 does not have: the diagonal is
an isometry but not a surjection, hence not unitary. It copies basis states
only, so it is a measurement basis rather than an illegal cloner -- but it still
has no adjoint that inverts it

inl, inr and zero go for the same dimensional reason. Writing ℂⁿ for the space of states of a type with n values, inl : a ↔ a + b embeds ℂⁿ in the bigger ℂⁿ⁺ᵐ, and zero is the zero matrix, which inverts nothing. join : a + a ↔ a maps down rather than up, and is the mirror image of the same problem. It was 42's only source of many-valued answers, so with it goes all nondeterminism.

#5.3 Where the restriction actually bites

A useful sanity check, because "not unitary" sounds more restrictive than it is. In finite dimensions, an isometry from a space to itself is automatically unitary, so the restriction has only two ways to catch you:

  1. The spaces differ in size: copy, inl, inr. Nothing can be done; these are not gates.
  2. The space is infinite-dimensional. This is the subtler one and it is why Q42 has no arithmetic on unbounded numbers; §8 is the worked case.

#5.4 What it means for clauses

A 42 clause definition compiles to the primitives above, so the ones Q42 drops take some of the clause rules with them. Each change is one of §5.1–5.3 again:

  • A name is used exactly once on each side. In 42 a name used twice in a term is a copy, and a name used twice in a pattern is an equality test, its

copy!. Neither is unitary (§5.2), so in Q42 both are errors.

  • The patterns cover every input exactly once, and the terms every output. In 42 a definition is the union of its clauses, overlap merely warns, and a missing case means no answer. In Q42 a definition is the sum of its clauses, which is §5.1's 1 + 1 = 2: overlap would add two matrices, and a gap would leave a partial map. Both are errors.
  • A let pattern cannot fail. A call's result may be a superposition, and a pattern that kept part of it and dropped the rest would not be unitary.

§7.3 shows what is left, which is most of what one wants.

#6. What Q42 adds

  • 6.1 The generators
  • 6.2 Phases are the sum functor
  • 6.3 Hadamard, and "up to phase"
  • 6.4 Control is dist, which you have already met
  • 6.5 Which equations hold, and how Q42 decides

Two primitives. Everything else in q42/gates.42 is built from them and from 42's existing plumbing.

#6.1 The generators

omega : 1 <-> 1            a scalar; an 8th root of 1
v     : 1+1 <-> 1+1        a square root of `swapsum`

omega acts on the unit type 1, which has a single value () and so a one-dimensional space: its "matrix" is a single number. That number is e^{iπ/4} = (1 + i)/√2, the eighth of a turn from the complex-number primer of §2. It is called an eighth root of 1 because multiplying it by itself eight times gives 1. On its own omega does nothing visible; combined with another gate (§6.2) it multiplies part of a state by that phase.

v acts on one qubit, and doing it twice is NOT: v ; v = swapsum, which is the gate x of §2.3. So it is "half a NOT", which has no classical meaning at all; no operation on bits, done twice, flips a bit. Its matrix is

V  =  1/2 × [ -1+i   -1-i ]
            [ -1-i   -1+i ]

and multiplying it by itself does give [[0, 1], [1, 0]]. (You never write these numbers in a Q42 program; you write omega and v, and Q42 knows their values.)

They satisfy three equations. Here omega^8 means omega composed with itself eight times, S is the gate id + (omega ; omega) that §6.2 explains (it multiplies |1> by i), and omega^2 . t means the gate t with its whole matrix multiplied by the number omega²:

(E1)  omega^8   =  id
(E2)  v ; v     =  swapsum
(E3)  v ; S ; v =  omega^2 . (S ; v ; S)

(E1) and (E2) only say what the two generators are: an eighth root of 1 and a square root of NOT. (E3) is the one that does the work: it is the one equation that ties v and omega together. Without it, omega = 1 would satisfy everything, and since a phase of 1 changes nothing, the language would collapse to classical reversible computing. With it, the Hadamard gate becomes expressible (§6.3), and that is what makes the language quantum.

And eighth is not a choice among many. Read (E3) as an equation in omega, with v the matrix above, and it has exactly two solutions: e^{iπ/4} and −e^{iπ/4}. Both are eighth roots of 1 and of no smaller root: the first power of either that equals 1 is the eighth (its order is 8). No solution has order 16, and no solution fails to be a root of 1 at all. (The two other eighth roots of order 8 satisfy (E3) only for the other square root of NOT, V's conjugate.) So a finer angle than an eighth of a turn cannot be had by relaxing an equation; it needs a further generator, which is the ladder §9.4 ends on.

#6.2 Phases are the sum functor

A phase gate multiplies |1> by a phase and leaves |0> alone. In 42's notation that is id + <something>, the sum functor acting on the right branch only: on L (), which is |0>, do id; on R (), which is |1>, do the something. Here the something is a power of omega:

def t = id + omega                              -- diag(1, e^{i pi/4})
def s = id + (omega ; omega)                    -- diag(1, i)
def z = id + (omega ; omega ; omega ; omega)    -- diag(1, -1)

diag(a, b) is the matrix with a and b on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere: |0> is multiplied by a and |1> by b. So t turns |1> an eighth of a turn, s a quarter (omega twice is i), and z half (omega four times is −1); z is the gate usually written Z.

$ 42q matrix gates s
s : qubit <-> qubit
     |0>  [  1   0 ]
     |1>  [  0  1i ]

Nothing was added to the language to express this. + already meant "switch on the label", and a phase gate is a switch that does nothing on one branch.

The three are one group. Look at what t, s and z have in common. Each is id + some power of omega, and by (E1) there are only eight such powers. Under multiplication they form a group: multiplying two of them gives another, and each has an inverse among them. It is a cyclic group of order eight, meaning all eight are powers of a single one, omega. A subgroup is a subset that is itself closed under multiplication, and the three gates above generate its three nontrivial subgroups:

z  is  id + omega^4   generates  {1, -1}                  order 2
s  is  id + omega^2   generates  {1, i, -1, -i}           order 4
t  is  id + omega     generates  all eight powers         order 8

Nobody chose those three. They are the subgroup chain of the group omega generates, written out in the language, and there is no fourth one to write.

This is what (E3) is for. §6.1 put its job negatively — without it omega could be id and the language would collapse to classical reversible computing. The useful way to say it is that (E3) fixes which group the phases form, and that single choice carries the physical content of the language.

Two names from the field make the point sharper. The Clifford gates are the ones built from h (§6.3), s and the controlled-NOT of §2.6. Their phases are exactly the order-four subgroup: s, z, and nothing finer. By the Gottesman–Knill theorem, any circuit made of Clifford gates can be simulated efficiently on an ordinary computer, entanglement and interference included (§9.3). Clifford+T adds t, and with it the order-eight phases, and it can not, as far as anyone knows. The step from s to t is the step from a theory a laptop can simulate to one it cannot, and in group terms it is one rung of that chain.

The idea has a name in the research literature. Coecke & Duncan attach to each observable, each quantity one can measure, the group of phases available to it, its phase group, and show that this one invariant is sharp enough to tell physical theories apart. Their example: quantum mechanics restricted to Clifford operations, and a classical model built by Spekkens to imitate it, agree about nearly everything, yet their phase groups differ, Z₄ (the four phases 1, i, −1, −i) against Z₂ × Z₂ (four elements, each its own inverse), and that difference is exactly where they disagree about the three-qubit version of the Bell state, (|000> + |111>)/√2 (ghz in gates.42): the real theory shows correlations, of the kind §2.6 described, that the imitation cannot. Q42's phase group is Z₈. RELATED §11.4 sets the two calculi side by side generator for generator.

#6.3 Hadamard, and "up to phase"

The Hadamard gate of §2.4 is built from x, s and v:

def hcore = x ; s ; v ; s ; x
def h     = unitprod! ; (omega * hcore) ; unitprod

hcore is already the Hadamard, except that its whole matrix is the Hadamard's multiplied by the phase omega⁻¹, an eighth of a turn backwards. h corrects that: unitprod! pairs the qubit with a (), omega * hcore multiplies by omega on the () side while running hcore on the qubit, and unitprod drops the () again. (x ; s ; v ; s ; x is a palindrome, so for once it reads the same in either direction.)

That extra factor is a global phase (§2.7): physically undetectable, since a phase on the whole state changes no measurement, which is why many gate identities in the literature hold only "up to phase", up to such a factor. Q42 tracks it exactly anyway, which is why h comes out as the textbook matrix on the nose rather than nearly:

$ 42q matrix gates h
h : qubit <-> qubit
     |0>  [  0.707107   0.707107 ]
     |1>  [  0.707107  -0.707107 ]

#6.4 Control is dist, which you have already met

A controlled gate applies its target gate only when the control qubit is |1>. That is a conditional, and 42's core does conditionals with dist (the 42 manual, §13). So:

def mat    = dist ; (unitprod + unitprod)       -- (1+1) x A  <->  A + A
def ctrl m = mat ; (id + m) ; mat!

mat turns a pair (control qubit, rest) into a labelled value: the rest, under the control's label. ctrl m then applies m on the R branch only, the one where the control was |1>, and mat! puts the pair back together. Every controlled gate is one line:

def cx    = ctrl x                              -- CNOT
def ccx   = ctrl cx                             -- Toffoli
def cswap = ctrl swap                           -- Fredkin

cx is the controlled-NOT of §2.6. ccx controls a controlled-NOT, so it flips its third qubit when both of the others are |1>: the Toffoli gate of §1. swap is swapprod, which exchanges two qubits, so cswap, the Fredkin gate, exchanges its second and third qubits when the first is |1>.

Written as clauses (§7.3), a controlled gate is two lines, one for each value of the control, and cx compiles to exactly ctrl x with mat written out:

def cx (L (), q)  <->  (L (), q)
def cx (R (), q)  <->  (R (), x q)

The inferred type says what control requires and nothing more:

ctrl : (a <-> a) -> (qubit x a <-> qubit x a)

the target must have the same type in and out (an endo-relation, in the jargon), because both branches of id + m have to meet the A + A that mat produces.

And its adjoint needs no definition. ctrl! m is ctrl m!, which in physics is (Ctrl U)† = Ctrl U†: the adjoint of a controlled gate is the controlled adjoint. That falls out of how ! treats a parameter and is not arranged.

#6.5 Which equations hold, and how Q42 decides

The three equations of §6.1 are rules for proving two programs equal, the way x + 0 = x is a rule of algebra. Two properties matter, and they are the ones a computer scientist asks of any proof system:

  • Sound: whatever the rules prove equal really is equal. That holds: two terms (E1)–(E3) prove equal always denote the same matrix.
  • Complete: whatever really is equal, the rules can prove equal. That holds only for some families of circuits, of which the best known are the Clifford gates of §6.2 and Clifford+T on at most two qubits. Beyond those, two Q42 terms can denote the same matrix with nothing derivable from (E1)–(E3) saying so. ccz, the Z gate controlled by two qubits (ctrl cz, with cz = ctrl z), is an example of where that starts.

What the language offers in place of a complete proof system is evaluation, and exact evaluation. Because the generators are fixed numbers, every amplitude a Q42 program can produce is built from integers, i and 1/√2 by adding, subtracting and multiplying: mathematicians write that set Z[1/√2, i]. Such a number can be stored exactly, as a few integers, the way a fraction can be stored as two. q42/exact.py evaluates in that set rather than in floating point, so comparing two terms decides whether they are equal instead of approximating: h ; h and id are the same matrix, not the same to twelve places, and an amplitude that cancels is absent rather than small. That is what 42q equal does:

$ 42q equal gates x x
x : qubit <-> qubit
x : qubit <-> qubit
  equal on 2 dimension(s)

$ 42q equal gates s t
s : qubit <-> qubit
t : qubit <-> qubit
  differ at row |1>, column |1>: 1i vs (0.707107+0.707107i)

s and t differ exactly where §6.2 says: on |1>, a quarter turn against an eighth.

RELATED §11 sets this against the ZX-calculus, a graphical language for quantum circuits which has rules complete for all of Clifford+T, and makes the opposite trade to get them.

#7.1 A qubit is 1 + 1

There is no qubit type. As in 42, a two-case choice carrying no data is 1 + 1, and the two cases are L () and R (). Q42 calls them |0> and |1> when printing, and a file may name the type:

type qubit = 1 + 1

which is a printing abbreviation and changes nothing (the 42 manual, §3.7).

#7.2 Registers and kets

Several qubits together are called a register, and in Q42 a register is a nested pair, to the right:

|abc>   is   (a, (b, c))

You write states as kets on the command line: "|0110>", or just "0110". Only basis states can be written down, which is deliberate. You make superpositions by applying a gate.

#7.3 Writing gates as clauses

A Q42 definition can be written as clauses, as in the 42 manual's Part I, and it compiles to the point-free core. In Q42 a name is a wire: a pattern names the qubits it takes apart, and a call names the qubits a gate acts on. The compiler routes them together and back, and routing costs no gates (§9.4). The first stage of teleport.42 (teleportation is §8.5; only this stage matters here):

def bell (q0, (q1, q2))  <->
    let q1       = h q1        in
    let (q1, q2) = cx (q1, q2) in
    (q0, (q1, q2))

reads as the circuit: Hadamard on q1, then a CNOT from q1 to q2. A name may be bound again once a gate has consumed it, so the wires keep their names through the circuit.

A controlled gate is a definition with one clause per value of the control:

$ 42q show teleport cx
cx   (L (), q) <-> (L (), q)
cx   (R (), q) <-> (R (), x q)
cx!  (L (), q) <-> (L (), q)
cx!  (R (), x q) <-> (R (), q)

The two cx! lines are the clauses turned round, which is the adjoint (the 42 manual, §5). Matching a qubit like this is quantum control, not a classical branch: a control in superposition matches both clauses, and both run, weighted by its amplitudes. That is §6.4's dist, written with names.

A classical function is its truth table. To use a function f from bits to bits inside a quantum program it has to be made reversible, and the standard way is a gate (x, y) ↦ (x, y XOR f(x)), called an oracle for f. For f(x) = not x, from Deutsch's algorithm (§8.4):

def orcnot (L (), y)  <->  (L (), x y)       -- f(0) = 1: flip y
def orcnot (R (), y)  <->  (R (), y)         -- f(1) = 0: leave y

The rules are 42's, tightened as §5.4 explains, and each has a message:

error: line 2: in `dup a <-> (a, a)`, `a` is used twice.  Q42 cannot copy a value: copying maps a space into a bigger one, so it is not unitary (QMANUAL section 5.2).

The others refuse a name matched twice, two clauses whose patterns (or terms) overlap, an input (or output) no clause covers, a let pattern that can fail, and a call inside a pattern. Every definition the rules accept is unitary.

Two things clauses do not do. They do not make superpositions: a definition built from clauses alone permutes basis states, and every genuinely quantum step, h, v, omega and what is built from them, enters as a call. And not every definition they accept can yet be emitted as a circuit: when a definition's inputs and outputs cannot be split the same way, it is compiled by a construction that rearranges labelled values whose branches hold different numbers of qubits, and no fixed set of wires can hold those (§9.4). It runs in the evaluator, and 42q emit says why it will not emit it. The design, and the proof that the compiled term means what the clauses say and is unitary, are in QSUGAR.md.

#7.5 Superpositions in, and why that needed new machinery

42 applies a program to one value at a time and unions the results. Q42 cannot quite do that, and the reason is entanglement.

A basis value like (a, b) is always a product: qubit a and qubit b, separately. So applying f * g to a basis value can be done componentwise, and Q42's evaluator does what 42's does there. Entanglement appears only when you add such results together, which happens when the input is already a superposition. So Q42 has one function 42 has no need for, apply_vec, which applies a gate to a whole state rather than to a basis value.

You see this in bell: applied to |00> it is a single column, but the answer is a state that no pair of single-qubit states could produce.

#7.6 Measuring the result

42q FILE GATE STATE shows you the state. 42q sample measures it, which is what a real device would do:

$ 42q sample gates bell "|00>"
bell |00>
    outcome   exact  drawn
    |00>   50.0%     63  ###############
    |11>   50.0%     37  ###############
  -- 100 shots, seed 0

Two columns, deliberately. exact is the Born probability |amplitude|² (§2.2), which only a simulator can know. drawn counts the outcomes of 100 shots: a shot is one run of the program followed by a measurement, and counts of shots are all a real machine would give you. Seeing them together is the point: it shows what measurement costs you.

--shots N sets how many draws, and --seed S fixes them so a transcript stays true. The probabilities never need renormalising: a Q42 program starts from a unit vector and every term is unitary, so they already sum to 1.

Measuring only some qubits. --bits takes the positions you care about, counting from 0, and reports the distribution over those alone:

$ 42q sample deutsch dnot "|01>" --bits 0
    outcome   exact  drawn
    |1_>  100.0%    100  ##############################

The _ marks a qubit that was not measured. This is §8.4's algorithm, Deutsch's, answering its question: one query, one qubit, and the answer is certain.

Measurement is terminal: it happens after the program, never inside it. That is a deliberate line rather than a limitation of the command, and §9.2 explains what it costs and why.

#7.7 From a problem to a circuit

The commands above take a gate that already exists. A real task starts further back, with a problem stated in ordinary terms, and ends with a circuit a quantum computer can run. Getting from one to the other takes five steps, and the running example here is the modular adder, (a, b) ↦ (a, (a + b) mod N) for a fixed N and a, b < N, the arithmetic that Shor's factoring algorithm (§9.1) is built from. The files are in casestudy/, and the case study gives the whole program and its results.

1. State the problem in 42. Write down what the answer should be, as a 42 program. The language is 42, not Q42, on purpose. A specification may work on unbounded numbers, use a value twice, give no answer for inputs it does not care about, and combine cases with |, and Q42 forbids all four (§5). Those freedoms are what keep a specification short enough to be obviously right:

def reduce = keep | wrap                 -- keep s if s < n, else subtract n

def modadd (a, (b, n))  <->
    let (a, n) = lt (a, n) in
    let (b, n) = lt (b, n) in
    (a, reduce (add (a, b), n))

lt passes a pair through when its first number is the smaller and gives no answer otherwise, so modadd is simply undefined unless a, b < n. 42 runs it:

$ 42 casestudy/spec modadd "(3, (4, 5))"
modadd(3, (4, 5)) =
  (3, (2, 5))
  -- 1 result

2. Write the program in Q42. Now decide how the answer is computed on a fixed number of qubits. This is design work, and nothing derives it from step 1. It means choosing how numbers are laid out in registers (§7.2), choosing a construction (here the standard one, built from ripple-carry adders that pass a carry bit along the register as column addition does), and choosing the ancillas: extra qubits that start at |0>, serve as scratch space, and must be returned to |0> at the end, since a qubit left holding a leftover value stays entangled with the result and spoils it (§2.6). A Q42 program that simply listed the specification's cases would also be correct, but compiled as written it becomes a circuit that tests every case separately, exponentially larger than a good construction.

The program's type says exactly how many qubits it needs, before anything runs. 42q type casestudy/modadd mod3 reports nine qubits for N = 3: two for each of a and b, and five ancillas.

3. Check the program against the specification. On every input the specification answers, run both: 42 runs the specification, Q42 runs the program, and the two must agree. Q42's amplitudes are exact (§6.5), so the check is that the program's output is exactly one basis state, with amplitude exactly 1, holding the specification's answer, with every ancilla back at |0>. Inputs the specification does not answer are not compared, which is the right notion of correctness when nothing else will ever be fed in. Separately, 42q unitary confirms that the program is reversible on its whole space.

To compare, the two need a common language for their values: the specification speaks of numbers, the program of qubits. An encoder says which basis state stands for which value, and it is one more 42 definition, written beside the specification:

def halve 0        <->  (L (), 0)              -- n  as  (n mod 2, n div 2)
def halve 1        <->  (R (), 0)
def halve (n + 2)  <->  let (b, h) = halve n in (b, h + 1)

def bits1 n  <->  let (b, 0) = halve n in b
def bits2 n  <->  let (b, h) = halve n in (b, bits1 h)

def enc3 (a, (b, 3))  <->  (bits2 a, (bits2 b, (L (), (L (), (bits2 0, L ())))))

bits2 writes a number below 4 as two qubits, least significant first, and enc3 lays out a, b and the five ancillas, all |0>, in the order mod3 takes them. 42q check then does the comparison:

$ 42q check casestudy/modadd mod3 casestudy/spec modadd --encode enc3
mod3 against modadd, through enc3
  [ok ] the program: 9 input(s) agree, exactly
  [ok ] the circuit: 9 input(s) agree (77 gate(s) on 9 qubit(s))
  of 512 basis states, 496 encode no input and 7 encode one modadd does not answer; not compared

It goes through every basis state of the program's input, and runs the encoder backwards on it, which is where 42's two directions earn their keep: a state that encodes no value at all, such as one with an ancilla set, is skipped, and so is a value the specification gives no answer for, such as a = 3. For each of the nine that remain, it runs the specification, encodes the answer forwards, and requires the program to produce exactly that basis state. The second line is step 5, below.

Nothing in 42q check is about adders: the program, the specification and the encoder are all arguments, and --decode takes a second encoder when the answers are laid out differently from the inputs. What is specific to a problem is the encoder, which fixes which qubits hold which value. The command has one real limit, and it follows from what a 42 specification can say. A specification speaks of values, not amplitudes, so the check applies to programs that behave classically on the inputs it covers, taking each one to a single basis state: arithmetic, oracles, and the other reversible classical parts of a quantum algorithm. A genuinely quantum program, such as the Hadamard gate or the quantum Fourier transform, produces a superposition, which no 42 specification can describe. Q42 writes such programs perfectly well (§9.1 lists them); it is only this way of checking them that does not apply. They are checked instead with 42q matrix, 42q unitary, and 42q equal against another version (§6.5). The command also reports two conditions when they fail: the encoder must not give two values for one basis state, and the specification must give one answer per input, since a program produces only one.

4. Emit the circuit. 42q emit turns the program into OpenQASM 3, the standard text format for circuits (§9.4), and --gates lists the same gates in a form that is easier to read:

$ 42q emit casestudy/modadd mod3 --gates
mod3 : 9 qubit(s), 77 gate(s)
  cx 0, 2
  cx 0, 5
  ccx 5, 2, 0
  ...

cx is the controlled NOT and ccx the Toffoli gate, which flips its third qubit when the first two are both 1 (§6.4); the numbers are qubit positions.

5. Check the circuit against the specification. Run the emitted gate list on the same inputs, with a simulator that shares no code with Q42, and compare with the specification again. 42q check does this in the same run, and it is the second line of its report. For arithmetic like this, whose gates only permute basis states, following one bit string through the gates is a complete simulation, and it works at any width. A circuit with genuinely quantum gates in it goes through tools/qasm_sim.py instead, one gate matrix at a time, which is exponential in the number of qubits, so 42q check does that only up to ten qubits and says so when it skips the circuit.

Why two checks. They catch different mistakes. Step 3 catches a wrong program: the construction does not compute what the specification says. Step 5 catches a wrong translation: the program is right, but emitting it changed its meaning. Step 5 alone would not tell you which of the two had failed, and step 3 alone would trust the emitter.

Is the specification necessary? No. Nothing requires steps 1, 3 and 5: a Q42 program can be written, emitted and run without a specification, and most programs, quantum or not, are written without a formal one. What takes its place in ordinary programming is testing, and Q42 has the lighter-weight versions of that too: 42q FILE GATE STATE on a few inputs, 42q unitary for reversibility, 42q equal against a version already trusted. The specification is the exhaustive form of something done informally anyway.

The balance tips further towards writing one here than in ordinary programming, for three reasons:

  • The check is complete, not a sample. For an ordinary program an executable specification still only allows testing, since the inputs never run out. A Q42 program has a register of fixed size, so 42q check covers every input the specification answers. The same effort buys a proof over the valid inputs rather than evidence.
  • Some quantum mistakes do not show in the answer. An ancilla left at |1> can leave the result register exactly right, so trying a few inputs and reading the result finds nothing wrong. But the stray qubit is entangled with the result (§2.6), and once the program is part of a larger algorithm it silently destroys the interference the algorithm depends on (§2.5).

42q check requires every ancilla back at |0>, so it catches the mistake.

  • Debugging on a quantum computer is much harder. A running machine cannot be paused to look at its state, a measurement gives one random outcome and destroys the rest (§7.6), and every run costs time and money. The habits of ordinary debugging, printing a value or stepping through, have no equivalent there, so a mistake caught before the circuit leaves the simulator is worth more.

The specification applies only where it can: to the parts of a program that behave classically. For the genuinely quantum parts there is no 42 specification to write.

After step 5 the OpenQASM goes to a standard toolchain such as Qiskit, which fits it to a particular machine's qubits and connections (§9.4). For the modular adder the case study compares the result with the same construction built in Qiskit: the two circuits are identical, gate for gate.

#8.1 Fibonacci: why the obvious version cannot exist

In a reversible functional language you would write Fibonacci over the natural numbers, fib : nat <-> nat * nat. Q42 cannot have that, and not for want of a feature.

nat is mu X. 1 + X, an infinite type. Its space is infinite-dimensional, and Q42 has only finite-dimensional spaces. Ask for a basis and it says so:

cannot enumerate a recursive type: it is infinite, and Q42 has no
infinite-dimensional spaces

Underneath that is something sharper. succ is inr. On nat, succ is injective, since no two numbers have the same successor, but not surjective, because zero is nobody's successor. In finite dimensions that combination is impossible (§5.3); in infinite dimensions it is the standard example of an isometry that is not unitary, called the unilateral shift. Its adjoint is not its inverse: shifting up then down gets you back, but down then up loses zero.

So: Q42 has no successor, hence no naturals, hence no unbounded arithmetic. This is what "unitary" means, not a gap in the implementation.

#8.2 What works: fix the width

Real quantum hardware has registers of fixed size and does arithmetic mod 2ⁿ. Do the same and the recurrence

(a, b)  |->  (b, a + b  mod 2^n)

becomes a permutation of the 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ basis states: no two registers go to the same place, because the step can be undone, a being recovered as (a + b) − b mod 2ⁿ. (Algebraically, the step is the matrix [[0,1],[1,1]], which has determinant −1, a number with an inverse mod 2ⁿ.) A permutation of basis states is a matrix of 0s and 1s with one 1 in each row and column, which is unitary, and therefore expressible. q42/fib.42 does it on two 2-bit registers, |x0 x1 y0 y1>, writing each step as the circuit it is (§7.3):

def add4 (x0, (x1, (y0, y1)))  <->               -- (x, y) -> (x, x+y mod 4)
    let (x0, (y0, y1)) = ccx (x0, (y0, y1)) in     -- Toffoli: x0, y0 -> y1
    let (x0, y0)       = cx (x0, y0)        in     -- CNOT:    x0 -> y0
    let (x1, y1)       = cx (x1, y1)        in     -- CNOT:    x1 -> y1
    (x0, (x1, (y0, y1)))

def swapregs (x0, (x1, (y0, y1)))  <->  (y0, (y1, (x0, x1)))

def fib = swapregs ; add4

In the comments, x0, y0 -> y1 means controls x0 and y0, target y1. add4 is three gates because y += x is "if x₀ then y += 1" followed by "if x₁ then y += 2". Adding 1 to the two-bit y = (y0, y1), low bit first, flips y0 and, if y0 was 1, carries into y1; controlled on x0, that is the Toffoli (carry: flip y1 if x0 and y0) followed by a CNOT (flip y0 if x0). Adding 2 flips y1 alone: the second CNOT. The Toffoli comes first because the carry depends on the old y0. swapregs is no gates at all: its clause only rearranges, and rearranging emits nothing.

Iterating gives the Fibonacci numbers mod 4:

step  0: register = (0, 1)     F_0  = 0
step  3: register = (2, 3)     F_3  = 2
step  5: register = (1, 0)     F_5  = 5,  mod 4 = 1
step 10: register = (3, 1)     F_10 = 55, mod 4 = 3

and since it is unitary, running it backwards recovers the seed exactly, rather than "a set containing the seed", which is what 42 would give you. Applying fib six times brings every register back to where it started, and no fewer times does (its order is 6). That matches number theory: the Fibonacci numbers mod m repeat with a fixed period, called the Pisano period of m, and the Pisano period of 4 is 6.

#8.3 The quantum part

Everything above is a permutation, so it is reversible classical computing and 42 could do it too. What Q42 adds is that the seed may be a superposition:

def hy0 (x0, (x1, (y0, y1)))  <->  let y0 = h y0 in (x0, (x1, (y0, y1)))
def qfib = hy0 ; fib

puts one bit of the seed through a Hadamard first. The register then holds two seeds at once, in superposition, and every later step advances both: two orbits, the sequences of registers each seed runs through, computed together. The whole thing still inverts exactly.

#8.4 Deutsch's algorithm, and what Q42 shows you

Fibonacci is arithmetic. Deutsch's algorithm is a real quantum algorithm, the smallest one there is and the first found to beat its classical rival.

The problem: you are handed a black box computing an unknown one-bit function f, and asked whether f is constant (f(0) = f(1)) or balanced (f(0) ≠ f(1)). Classically you must evaluate f twice, since one evaluation tells you nothing about the other. Deutsch's algorithm answers after one.

The black box has to be reversible like everything else, so it is an oracle in the sense of §7.3, (x, y) ↦ (x, y XOR f(x)): it keeps x and XORs f(x) into a second qubit. That is the Toffoli trick from §1, which is also mul keeping its multiplier in the 42 manual's §9.3. There are only four one-bit functions, so q42/deutsch.42 writes all four out, each as its truth table (§7.3):

def orc0 (a, y)     <->  (a, y)              -- f(x) = 0       constant

def orc1 (a, y)     <->  (a, x y)            -- f(x) = 1       constant

def orcid (L (), y)   <->  (L (), y)         -- f(x) = x       balanced
def orcid (R (), y)   <->  (R (), x y)

def orcnot (L (), y)  <->  (L (), x y)       -- f(x) = not x   balanced
def orcnot (R (), y)  <->  (R (), y)

The algorithm is three steps: superpose both inputs, query the oracle once, and interfere the results back together. h * h applies a Hadamard to each of the two qubits, and h * id to the first only:

def d0 = (h * h) ; orc0 ; (h * id)

Now run each of the four, and watch the first qubit:

$ 42q deutsch d0 "|01>"
d0 |01>
  = 0.707107|00>  +  -0.707107|01>

$ 42q deutsch dnot "|01>"
dnot |01>
  = -0.707107|10>  +  0.707107|11>

For d0, every term begins |0. For dnot, every term begins |1. The other two behave the same way as their partner: the first qubit is |0> for a constant f and |1> for a balanced one, with certainty. One query, and the answer is not probable but definite.

And it will say so outright if you measure the first qubit (§7.6):

$ 42q sample deutsch dnot "|01>" --bits 0
    outcome   exact  drawn
    |1_>  100.0%    100  ##############################

Why it works. Follow the amplitudes from the input |01>. The two Hadamards give

1/2 ( |0> + |1> ) ( |0> - |1> )  =  0.5|00> - 0.5|01> + 0.5|10> - 0.5|11>

Now the oracle XORs f(x) into the second qubit. XORing 1 into |0> − |1> gives |1> − |0>, the same with its sign flipped; XORing 0 leaves it alone. So the second qubit is unchanged except for a sign, −1 exactly when f(x) = 1, and that sign ends up on the first qubit's term for that x:

1/2 ( s0 |0> + s1 |1> ) ( |0> - |1> )        s0 = (-1)^f(0),  s1 = (-1)^f(1)

If f is constant, s0 = s1 and the first qubit is ±(|0> + |1>)/√2; the last Hadamard turns that into ±|0> (§2.4 read backwards). If f is balanced, s0 = −s1, the first qubit is ±(|0> − |1>)/√2, and the Hadamard turns it into ±|1>. One query decided a property of both values of f, because the query was made on a superposition of both inputs and the answer came back as a sign, which the final Hadamard turns into a bit by interference (§2.5).

That is why the state is the more instructive output. A device hands you one bit and hides the mechanism. Q42 shows you the amplitudes, so you can see the second qubit's +/-0.707107, the |0> − |1> that carried the sign, and the first qubit holding the answer. For understanding why an algorithm works, as opposed to what it returns, that is worth more than a sample. §9.1 returns to this.

#8.5 Teleportation, without measuring in the middle

Teleportation moves the state of one qubit to another qubit, possibly far away, without sending the qubit itself, using a shared entangled pair and two classical bits. The protocol: Alice holds a qubit q0 in some state she wants to send, which she need not know. Alice and Bob each hold one half of a Bell pair (§2.6), q1 and q2. Alice applies a CNOT from q0 to q1 and a Hadamard to q0, measures both her qubits, and sends Bob the two bits she got. Bob applies X (NOT) to q2 if the second bit is 1, and Z (§6.2: multiply |1> by −1) if the first is 1. After that, q2 holds exactly the state q0 started in.

It is the textbook example of an algorithm that requires measuring half way through, and Q42 has no mid-circuit measurement (§9.2), so this ought to be impossible.

It is not, and the reason is a theorem. The principle of deferred measurement (Nielsen & Chuang §4.4; their Quantum Computation and Quantum Information is the standard textbook) says that any circuit with mid-circuit measurements and classically-controlled gates can be rewritten with every measurement moved to the end, each classical control replaced by a quantum control. Q42 has quantum control, a clause that matches on a qubit (§7.3), so the rewritten circuit is an ordinary Q42 definition.

q42/teleport.42 is that circuit, on (q0, (q1, q2)), and the deferral is visible in one stage:

def bell (q0, (q1, q2))  <->                       -- entangle q1 with q2
    let q1       = h q1        in
    let (q1, q2) = cx (q1, q2) in
    (q0, (q1, q2))

def alice (q0, (q1, q2))  <->                      -- CNOT q0->q1, then H on q0
    let (q0, q1) = cx (q0, q1) in
    let q0       = h q0        in
    (q0, (q1, q2))

def bob (q0, (q1, q2))  <->                        -- CNOT q1->q2, CZ q0->q2
    let (q1, q2) = cx (q1, q2) in
    let (q0, q2) = cz (q0, q2) in
    (q0, (q1, q2))

def tele = bell ; alice ; bob

cz is z controlled on its first qubit. bob is where "if Alice's bit is 1, apply X" became a CNOT, and "if the other bit is 1, apply Z" a controlled-Z: the same conditionals, controlled by the qubit rather than by a bit read out of it.

It is unitary like everything else:

$ 42q unitary teleport tele --qubits 3
  [ok ] tele ; tele! = id on 8 dimension(s)

Sending |0> proves little, so check prepares a real state on q0, a superposition with a phase (prep = h ; t), teleports it, and applies prep!, the preparing gate's inverse, to q2. If the state genuinely arrived, amplitudes and phase intact, q2 must return to |0> with certainty:

$ 42q sample teleport check "|000>" --bits 2
    |__0>  100.0%    100  ##############################

It does. The deferred measurements have not vanished: they are the other two qubits, which end up spread evenly over all four outcomes, exactly the four results Alice would have read. (checkh is check with h as the preparing gate, so that the amplitudes print simply.)

$ 42q teleport checkh "|000>"
  = 0.5|000>  +  0.5|010>  +  0.5|100>  +  0.5|110>

So the canonical mid-circuit algorithm runs here today, unchanged and unitary. That is the strongest single argument in §9.2 for leaving the language alone.

#9.1 What it is for

A fair reading of a list of limitations is "then what use is it?", so the useful part first.

Expressiveness. omega and v generate Clifford+T (§6.2). That gate set is universal: any unitary can be approximated by a Clifford+T circuit to any precision you like, and Clifford+T circuits themselves are represented exactly. The building blocks of the famous algorithms are all writable: the step of Grover's search (§2.5) that amplifies the right answer, the arithmetic modulo N inside Shor's factoring algorithm, and the quantum Fourier transform, the quantum version of the discrete Fourier transform and the core of Shor's. §8.4 writes out Deutsch's algorithm in six lines.

These are genuinely quantum programs, using superposition, interference and entanglement, not classical ones run on qubits, and the repository has them as files: the Hadamard gate h and the entangling bell and ghz in q42/gates.42, Deutsch's algorithm in q42/deutsch.42, teleportation in q42/teleport.42, Grover's search in q42/grover.42, and the quantum Fourier transform on three qubits, exactly, in q42/qft3.42. What Q42 leaves out is measuring in the middle of a program (§9.2), not quantum behaviour.

The Fourier transform shows the one qualification. Q42's phases are eighth turns (§6.2), and the transform on four or more qubits also wants a sixteenth turn, and finer ones as it grows, which no single Q42 gate gives. qft.42, a 42 program that generates the transform for any width, therefore leaves those small rotations out, giving the approximate transform, which is also what hardware usually runs. Where the exact one is needed, a finer rotation can be approximated by a longer sequence of Q42 gates to whatever precision is wanted, about a hundred gates for one part in 10¹⁰, which is the universality above at work. So every quantum program can be written: some angles exactly, the rest to a chosen precision, which is the trade-off error-corrected hardware makes too (§9.4).

Gate identities, checked mechanically. What Carette et al.'s calculus (§4.2; they call it √Π) contributes is an equational theory, a set of rules for proving two circuits equal (§6.5), and Q42 makes it executable. 42q law checks that ! really is the adjoint; 42q unitary checks t ; t! = id across a whole library; 42q matrix prints the matrix so you can compare. Gate identities like H ; H = id, H ; X ; H = Z, S ; S = Z and (Ctrl U)† = Ctrl U†: all of these can be run, and each is a test in tests/test_q42.py.

Amplitudes and samples, side by side. 42q sample measures a result as a device would, and prints the exact Born probabilities next to the draw (§7.6). So Deutsch's question gets an answer, |1_> 100.0%, while the state that produced it stays visible: -0.707107|10> + 0.707107|11>, from which you can see why the first qubit is certainly |1>. A real machine gives you only the right-hand column, and for learning or debugging a circuit, having both is worth more than either.

Uncomputation as a term. It is P ; Q ; P!, and because every Q42 term is invertible, P! really is P's inverse — the undo is derived from the forward term rather than written a second time and trusted to match.

Be accurate about the comparison, though, because it is easy to overstate. Silq's contribution is safe, automatic uncomputation: its point is that the programmer never decides where the undo goes. Q42 is in the category Silq set out to improve on, the one where you trigger it yourself — as Quipper (a quantum language embedded in Haskell) does with with_computed, and Q# (Microsoft's quantum language) with within … apply. Those two derive the inverse from the forward code as well, so that is not what separates them. What Q42 adds is that the inverse exists for every term, with no declaration and no check that the term happens to avoid measurement, because no term can contain one (§9.5 says this more carefully). That is a smaller claim than Silq's, and a different one.

Evidence for a claim about language design. q42/ shares the data types for values and programs (Value, Term), the parser and the whole type checker with rel42/, and q42.dagger is rel42.dagger, the same function object. The claim being demonstrated is that 42's primitives are not a curated set but a forced one, so that reaching quantum computing is a change of number system rather than a new language. Q42 existing, and being this small, is the argument.

What the change of semiring costs. Worth saying beside the claims it qualifies. 42's unusual property is the one §5 removed: its programs denote relations and may give many answers, in both directions at once. Unitaries are bijections, so Q42 does not have it — §4 puts Q42 where Π is, and the property that separated 42 from the other reversible languages is exactly the one the change of number system gives up. Nothing above depends on it. The case for Q42 is the paragraphs above; it is not that Q42 inherited what made 42 unusual, because it did not.

#9.2 Where the language stops

Q42 is a language for the unitary layer of quantum computing, the part made of gates, with measurement at its edge. That sentence is the boundary, and it is chosen rather than unfinished: it is what buys every property in §9.1.

Inside the line. Every term denotes a unitary, so ! is defined on every term with no side conditions, t ; t! = id holds by construction, and 42q unitary can assert it over a whole file. Measurement sits at the end: 42q sample applies the Born rule to the state the program produced (§7.6). That is an operation on the output, not a term in the language, which is exactly why it costs the language nothing.

Outside the line: measuring half way through, branching on the result, and carrying on. Q42 has no such term and is not going to acquire one. Measurement is not unitary, being neither invertible nor deterministic, so a measure term would be one for which ! has no answer, and every property in the paragraph above would become conditional: unitary except where a measurement appears. The literature reaches the same conclusion and treats measurement as a layer over a unitary core rather than a primitive inside it. The construction is Carette et al., The Quantum Effect, and it is worth seeing how much machinery it takes. Measurement there is not a primitive but a derived term, measure_Z = copy_Z ≫ fst: copy in the standard basis (§5.2's copy), then keep only the first copy (≫ is their composition). Keeping one copy needs a way to discard the other, and that arrives only with a further layer of their construction, which hides what is thrown away. And the resulting model is one of partial maps, landing not among unitaries but among contractions, linear maps on the spaces of states of §2 that never make a vector longer. The authors are candid about the cost, noting that their discard postulate "is dangerous, as it does not enforce that it is only applied to total maps".

That is the formal content of the line drawn here. Adding measurement is not an addition to a unitary language; it is leaving the unitary category. Q42 stays.

The line also costs no expressiveness. The principle of deferred measurement (Nielsen & Chuang §4.4) is a theorem: any circuit that measures mid-way and applies gates conditioned on the classical outcome can be rewritten with the measurements moved to the end and the control made quantum, which is what ctrl does. So the missing construct is a convenience of execution rather than a source of computational power.

q42/teleport.42 is that theorem made concrete. Quantum teleportation is the example usually offered as the reason a language needs mid-circuit measurement, and it runs here unchanged, as a single unitary on three qubits:

$ 42q unitary teleport tele --qubits 3
  [ok ] tele ; tele! = id on 8 dimension(s)

Alice's two classical bits are never extracted; her four possible outcomes stay in superposition and Bob's corrections are applied with ctrl instead. §8.5 works through it, including the check that a state carrying a genuine phase arrives intact.

The same holds for Shor's algorithm, which is often assumed to need mid-circuit measurement and does not: its quantum part is one unitary followed by one measurement, and what comes after (a classical calculation, the continued-fraction step, that turns the measured value into a factor, and a retry if it fails) is ordinary classical code around that subroutine. Q42 hands you the measured value and a script does the rest.

What is genuinely on the far side of the line is narrower than it first looks: error correction (§2.7), where an error's signature, its syndrome, is measured and acted on repeatedly while the computation continues; measurement-based computing, a model in which the whole computation is done by measuring qubits of a large entangled state, each measurement chosen from the results of earlier ones; and qubit reuse, measuring a qubit to reset it for another use, which matters when hardware has a fixed qubit budget and not at all in a simulator. None of these is what Q42 is for.

Two smaller boundaries follow from the same place:

  • No mid-program state preparation. A Q42 program is a unitary on a fixed space, so there is no conjuring a fresh |0> half way through; that would change the dimension. Inputs come from the command line instead.
  • No noise. No decoherence, no gate fidelities, no error model. Everything here is the idealised unitary layer.

#9.3 The comparison that is not the right one

An n-qubit gate is a 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ matrix, so running Q42 costs exponential time and memory. It is worth being precise about what that does and does not mean.

It does not distinguish Q42 from other quantum languages. Every classical simulator of quantum circuits pays this, including those of Qiskit, IBM's widely used quantum toolkit; it is a fact about simulating quantum mechanics on a classical machine, not about this implementation. A quantum language is not in competition with quantum hardware: Q42's peers are Silq, Π and Quipper, not a chip.

There is one genuine nuance. By the Gottesman–Knill theorem, circuits built only from Clifford gates (h, s, cx) are efficiently simulable classically, despite looking thoroughly quantum, entanglement and interference included. So "quantum-looking" and "hard to simulate" are not the same property. It is t that takes a circuit beyond Clifford, and Q42 has t. §6.2 says the same thing as a fact about groups rather than about simulation: s generates the order-four phase group and t the order-eight one, and the boundary Gottesman–Knill draws falls between them.

In summary: Q42 is a language and a proof-checker for the unitary layer of quantum computing, complete for that layer, with measurement at its edge (§9.2) and simulation cost as the price everybody pays.

#9.4 Could a Q42 program run on real hardware?

It can, once machines are stable and large enough: 42q emit writes any definition out as OpenQASM 3, a standard text format for quantum circuits, which every mainstream toolchain reads. This section says why the language sits well for that, what the emitter does, and where the real limit is.

The language is well placed for it. The machines that will run long computations must correct their own errors (§2.7), and the leading scheme for that, the surface code, makes Clifford gates cheap and the t gate expensive. Such machines are therefore built around Clifford+T, and the cost of a program on them is counted by its T-count, the number of t gates. A Q42 program already is a circuit in exactly that gate set, since omega and v generate Clifford+T. Nothing needs translating into another model of computation.

Better, most of the language costs nothing to execute. Classifying each primitive by what it does to the underlying bits:

primitiveeffect on the bitson hardware
id, assocsum, assocprod, unitsum, unitprod, distnothing at allfree

Only three of the ten are physical operations, and they are exactly X, phase and √X. Everything else is the semiring plumbing of §3.5, and plumbing is compile-time bookkeeping: assocprod regroups a × (b × c) into (a × b) × c and the bit string is untouched, while unitsum removes a tag whose other branch is uninhabited, 0 + qubit and qubit being both two-dimensional, so there was never a bit there to move.

Inlining the library bears this out:

  gate     structural  physical   which physical operations
  ccx              14         1   {swapsum: 1}
  cswap             7         1   {swapprod: 1}
  bell             12         9   {omega: 5, swapsum: 3, v: 1}

A Toffoli is fourteen structural steps and one real gate. Every ctrl is dist-plumbing wrapped round a single operation, which is a direct consequence of the primitives being the semiring isomorphisms rather than a hand-picked set.

Not every type is a qubit register. a + b has dimension dim(a) + dim(b), which need not be a power of two: 1 + (1 + 1) is a qutrit, a perfectly good three-dimensional space with no qubit realisation. Q42 itself does not care, because it evaluates in any finite dimension; a compiler would, and the condition it needs is not "is the dimension a power of two" but a recursive width:

width(1)      = 0
width(a x b)  = width(a) + width(b)
width(a + b)  = 1 + width(a),  provided width(a) = width(b)

a sum is a register exactly when both branches are the same width, and then the tag is one qubit and the branches share the rest. That correctly accepts (1+1) + (1+1) as two qubits and rejects 1 + (1+1). It is q42.types.width, and it returns None for the types that are not registers, among them 0, a variable and a mu, the last because Q42 has no infinite-dimensional spaces.

In practice it does not bite: every non-combinator definition in every Q42 library is register-shaped, across gates.42, fib.42, deutsch.42, teleport.42, grover.42, gsum.42, qft3.42 and classical.42, with none rejected. Programs people actually write in this language are already circuits.

Measurement. Terminal measurement is part of the tooling today (§7.6): run the unitary, then sample the result. It is the Born rule and a random number, nothing more. It covers every algorithm whose last step is a single measurement: Deutsch (§8.4), Bernstein–Vazirani (which finds a hidden bit string with a single query), Grover, and the quantum subroutine of Shor.

$ 42q sample deutsch dnot "|01>" --bits 0
    |1_>  100.0%    100

Mid-circuit measurement is outside the language by design (§9.2), and by the deferred measurement principle a compiler can always work without it: the circuit it would emit is the one Q42 already describes, with quantum control where a device might have used a classical wire. What that trade costs on real hardware, and what a compiler could do about it, is the last paragraph below.

An emitter, not a backend. Q42 writes circuits; it does not drive machines, and the distinction is smaller than "no backend" makes it sound. The hard parts of getting a circuit onto a device are commodity. Those are routing, because real hardware cannot apply a two-qubit gate to an arbitrary pair, and rotation synthesis, because omega is only an eighth root of unity, so finer angles must be approximated into many T gates. Any existing quantum compiler does them for anything handed over as OpenQASM 3 or QIR (Quantum Intermediate Representation, another exchange format). They are not Q42's to write.

What is Q42's own is the lowering, and it is short. Two parts:

  • wire assignment: which physical qubit is which position in the nested pair, the width rule above read constructively, plus tracking swapprod as a permutation carried to the end so that it costs nothing instead of three CNOTs. That is exactly what q42/emit.py does, and it is why seven of the ten primitives emit no gate at all: id, assocprod, unitprod and dist re-bracket, swapprod is carried in the layout, and assocsum and unitsum cannot occur at a register type in the first place. Only swapsum, omega and

v produce output.

  • depth bounding: a circuit is finite, so a recursive definition must unroll to a fixed depth, and the emitter does not check that it does; it stops on a depth budget rather than proving termination.

Control needs no rule of its own, which is worth knowing: at a type a + b the first qubit is the tag, and f + g means f under tag 0 and g under tag 1. From that alone, cx, ccx and the Fredkin come out as single gates, and t = id + omega derives as the T gate, a controlled global phase being a relative one. The emitter never mentions ctrl, which is right, ctrl being a library definition rather than a language feature.

Every definition in every Q42 library emits, and each is checked against Q42's own matrix by a simulator in tools/qasm_sim.py that deliberately shares no code with the evaluator. What comes out is unoptimised, with z leaving as four eighth-turn phases, because correctness came first and the transpiler, the optimising pass of the downstream compiler, folds most of it.

There is also one piece that is not commodity, because no previous language needed it. Q42 defers every measurement (§9.2), and on hardware deferral is not free: it spends coherence time, the short while a qubit keeps its state before decoherence (§2.7) spoils it, and forbids reusing a qubit. So a serious lowering would want to run the deferred measurement principle backwards: recognise a ctrl whose control qubit is never touched again, and emit measure-and-branch where the term says quantum control. That is a rewrite over a program Q42 already expresses, not a feature the language lacks.

In summary, Q42 is a plausible front end and not a plausible toolchain, which is the shape to aim at rather than a shortfall. Its semantics are the right ones, its plumbing is free, its libraries all describe genuine registers, and the measurement it does not have is one a compiler can do without.

It translates into a circuit format the rest of the world already compiles, and the emitter's own gaps are the two small ones named above. The remaining limit is the gate set. omega is an eighth root of unity, so the unitaries Q42 can write down are exactly those over Z[1/√2, i] — Clifford+T, and nothing finer. That is not a limit on what Q42 can compute: Clifford+T is universal, and any rotation is reachable to precision ε with a T-count logarithmic in 1/ε. It is a limit on what Q42 can name. A phase gate turning |1> by π/7, written Rz(π/7) in the literature, must arrive already approximated by Clifford+T, where a device with native rotations would have applied it as one control pulse.

Against the machines this section opened with, that is not a shortfall at all: on a surface code arbitrary rotations are the expensive thing, and being confined to the distilled set is what makes T-count meaningful in the first place. It is a shortfall only against today's devices, which are not the target.

Nor is that an artefact of the surface code. Eastin–Knill: for any code, the operations that can be applied to each physical qubit independently — the only ones that do not let one error breed into many — form a finite set, and a finite set is never enough to compute with. Every protected machine therefore has a limited cheap alphabet and a discrete expensive extra. A cheap continuum of angles belongs to the unprotected era, not the mature one.

Lifting it would buy device-generality. It would not falsify §6 — (E1), (E2) and (E3) go on holding — it would end their sufficiency: two constants would no longer generate, and phases would become an indexed family rather than a primitive. A Q42 with a 16th, 32nd, … root of 1 added at each level (a cyclotomic Q42, in the number theorist's word) would keep exact evaluation and decidable equality and lose only the finite presentation: the fixed, finite list of generators and equations. Going the whole way, to a continuum of angles, loses exactness itself, and with it the property that a Q42 program has a matrix rather than an approximation to one. That last trade is the one the language is made of.

Read beside §6.2, the three arguments this section has made turn out to be one. The phases of Q42 form Z₈, the cyclic group of the eight eighth-turns, a finite subgroup of the circle of all phases. A cyclotomic Q42 is a larger finite subgroup, Z₁₆ or Z₃₂. The continuum is the circle itself. And Eastin–Knill is the statement that no machine which corrects its errors can offer you the circle: its cheap alphabet is finite, so it sits somewhere on that ladder and never at the top. So why eight, what would lifting cost, and why is the restriction not an artefact of one architecture are one question asked three times — which finite subgroup of the phase group do you take, and what does the choice buy — and Q42's answer is the smallest rung at which the language is universal at all, with v and the plumbing held fixed.

#9.5 Q42 beside other quantum languages

Several languages exist for programming quantum computers. They differ less in which gates they offer, since nearly all reach a universal set, than in what a program is, and that one difference explains most of the others. Six are worth knowing:

  • Qiskit (IBM) and Cirq (Google) are Python libraries. A program is an ordinary Python program whose calls append gates to a circuit object, which is then sent to a simulator or a device. They are the most widely used, and they run on real hardware today.
  • Q# (Microsoft) is a standalone language with its own compiler. A quantum routine is an operation; qubits are allocated with use, and measuring one is an ordinary operation, M, whose result the program can branch on.
  • Quipper (Green, Lumsdaine, Ross, Selinger & Valiron, 2013) is embedded in Haskell. A program is a Haskell function that generates a circuit, which lets it describe circuits far too large to run and count what they would cost.
  • Silq (Bichsel, Baader, Gehr & Vechev, ETH Zürich, 2020) is a standalone language whose type system works out where temporary qubits must be uncomputed (§1), so that the programmer never writes the undo.
  • Guppy (Quantinuum) is embedded in Python and designed for measuring in the middle of a program and branching on the result while the qubits are still live, which Quantinuum's trapped-ion machines support.
  • OpenQASM 3 is not a language you would write algorithms in but a text format for circuits, gate by gate: the common output of the others, and what

42q emit writes (§9.4).

What a program is. In Qiskit, Cirq, Guppy and Quipper there are two languages at once: the host language (Python or Haskell), which runs on your computer and decides which gates to emit, and the circuit it emits, which is what runs on the quantum machine. Loops, recursion and conditionals belong to the first; only a flat list of gates reaches the second. Q# and Silq have loops of their own, and their compilers turn a program into the gates that run. In Q42 a term is the circuit, ; and * and + are its only ways of combining things, and its meaning is a single matrix (§3.4). A loop that produces circuits of every width (a family) is not a Q42 term, any more than it is a Qiskit circuit. In Q42 the loop is written in 42: qft.42 is a recursive 42 program that takes a width and produces the quantum Fourier transform at that width as data, and tools/unquote.py turns that data into Q42 source, which 42q unitary then checks like any other library. So Q42 has a host language like the others, and it is the language Q42 was made from. RELATED.md §11.6 discusses this.

Measurement. Every other language here can measure a qubit half way through and choose what to do next from the result; for Guppy that is the point. Q42 measures only at the end, with 42q sample (§7.6), which also shows the exact probabilities beside the draw. It cannot measure half way through, by design, and §9.2 gives the reason (a term that measures has no inverse) and the theorem that makes the omission cost no computing power.

The inverse. Most of these languages can produce the inverse of a quantum routine for you: Qiskit's inverse(), Q#'s Adjoint, Quipper's reverse_generic. But each must first make sure the routine has no measurement in it, or no other step without an inverse, and refuses otherwise; in Q# you declare is Adj and the compiler holds you to it. In Q42 ! applies to every term without a declaration, because no term can contain such a step. The same holds for control: Q#'s Controlled turns an operation into its controlled version when the operation permits it, Q42's ctrl (§6.4) does so for every term.

Deciding equality. To learn whether two Qiskit circuits do the same thing, you simulate both in floating point and compare the matrices up to a tolerance, so "equal" means "equal to twelve places". Q42's generators are fixed numbers, so its amplitudes can be stored exactly, and 42q equal decides the question, with no tolerance (§6.5). Q42 also has equations for proving two terms equal without computing their matrices, but §6.5 is candid that they are complete only for some families of circuits. A complete set for all of Clifford+T belongs to the ZX-calculus, a graphical notation in which circuit diagrams are rewritten into one another; ZX is a notation for reasoning, though, not a language you run.

In short, the mainstream languages are built to run programs on the machines that exist, with every convenience a host language gives. Q42 is built so that every program has a single meaning that can be inverted, controlled and compared exactly, and it pays for that with no mid-program measurement. RELATED.md §8 and §11 place it against these languages, and against verification languages like QWIRE and Qunity, in more detail.

To see the comparison on a real program, the case study writes the modular adder at the heart of Shor's algorithm in Q42, checks it on every valid input against a readable specification in 42, and builds the same circuit in Qiskit. The two come out identical, gate for gate, before and after Qiskit's optimiser. The case study is also candid about what that does not show: Q42's checks are exact, but a Qiskit user can test the same inputs, and the cheapest known adder relies on measuring mid-circuit, which Q42 excludes.

#Appendix: a program and its circuit

§9.4 says a Q42 definition lowers to OpenQASM 3 and that most of the language costs nothing to execute. This appendix does it once, in full, on the smallest program worth the trouble.

#The program

bell prepares the entangled pair of §2.6. Two operators:

$ 42q show gates bell
bell   = h * id ; cx
bell!  = cx! ; h! * id!

Read it as: Hadamard the left qubit, leave the right one alone, then a controlled-NOT. The adjoint was not written by anyone; it is the same definition read backwards. Written as clauses, bell (a, b) <-> let a = h a in cx (a, b) compiles to exactly this term.

#The circuit

$ 42q emit gates bell
OPENQASM 3.0;
include "stdgates.inc";

qubit[2] q;

gphase(0.78539816339744828);
x q[0];
p(0.78539816339744828) q[0];
p(0.78539816339744828) q[0];
gphase(2.3561944901923448);
p(1.5707963267948966) q[0];
h q[0];
p(1.5707963267948966) q[0];
p(0.78539816339744828) q[0];
p(0.78539816339744828) q[0];
x q[0];
cx q[0], q[1];

Twelve instructions for a two-operator program, and one qubit register of exactly the width the type demands. To read them: qubit[2] q declares two qubits, q[0] and q[1]; x is NOT and h the Hadamard; p(θ) is the phase gate diag(1, e^{iθ}) of §6.2; gphase(θ) multiplies the whole state by e^{iθ}, a global phase (§2.7); and cx q[0], q[1] is a CNOT with control q[0] and target q[1]. Angles are in radians, so 0.785… is π/4, an eighth of a turn, and 1.570… is π/2, a quarter.

#Why twelve

Because h is not a primitive here. Q42 has ten primitives (§10), and the Hadamard is a definition over two of them:

def hcore = x ; s ; v ; s ; x
def h     = unitprod! ; (omega * hcore) ; unitprod

with s = id + (omega ; omega). Expand that and the emitted sequence reads off in order:

in the termemitted
omegagphase(π/4)
xx q[0]
stwo p(π/4), an eighth turn each
vgphase, p(π/2), h, p(π/2) — the square root of NOT
stwo more p(π/4)
xx q[0]
cxcx q[0], q[1]

Nothing is looked up in a table of gate translations. The circuit is what the term says once the definitions are expanded, which is why the T-count of a Q42 program is a property of the program rather than of the compiler.

#What did not emit

Three things in that term produce no instruction at all:

  • the id in h * id, which is the right qubit being left alone;
  • both halves of the unitprod sandwich, which change how the value is bracketed and not what any bit holds;
  • the ctrl inside cx, which is mat ; (id + m) ; mat! and becomes the control structure of the cx line rather than an instruction of its own.

That is §9.4's claim about plumbing, visible: seven of the ten primitives emit nothing, so the instruction count tracks the quantum content of a term and not its size.

#Reading it against the language

The wire view drops the QASM boilerplate and says which qubits each gate lands on:

What the emitter guarantees is not brevity but agreement. tests/test_emit.py takes every definition in every library that is a circuit at all — a parameterised one like ctrl is not, until it is applied — emits it, reads the result back with a simulator in tools/qasm_sim.py that shares no code with the evaluator, and checks that matrix against the one Q42 computes from the term. For bell that is the 4×4 matrix sending |00> to (|00> + |11>)/√2.
