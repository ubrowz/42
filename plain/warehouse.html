A paper on reversible programming, retold

Running the Warehouse Backwards

The same argument as the technical paper — same sections, same claims, same proofs — with every piece of algebra replaced by something you can picture on a loading dock.

What this is

The paper being retold is called Reversibility without Injectivity. It is about a programming language, called 42, whose programs can be run backwards as easily as forwards. That is a small field with about six competing languages in it, and the paper's claim is that all six have made the same design decision, that the decision is a mistake, and that undoing it makes everything simpler rather than harder.

None of that requires knowing any mathematics. It requires being able to think about a process with steps in it, and about what it takes to retrace one. So this version is written around a warehouse: goods arrive, procedures act on them, and the question throughout is whether you can work out where something came from by knowing where it ended up.

Start with Part zero. The paper assumes you already know why anyone would want a program that runs backwards, why that turns out to be difficult, and why the language looks so peculiar — so it never explains any of them. Part zero is not in the paper and does all three in about five minutes.

Section numbering, figures, propositions and proofs are all where they are in the original, so if you ever want to look at the real thing the two documents line up paragraph for paragraph.

There is a companion to this one. The same language, read with a different kind of arithmetic, turns out to be a quantum programming language, and that is the subject of a second paper — retold the same way in When Yes Becomes a Number, which opens by explaining what a quantum computer actually does. Read this one first.

Part zero  ·  not in the paperWhy anyone would run a program backwards

The paper opens at full speed, with six competing languages and the constraint they share. Before that is readable, four things need saying: why running backwards is wanted at all, why it turns out to be hard, why this particular language looks so unlike ordinary programming, and what the single design question in the field is. None of it is in the paper, because the paper is written for people who already know.

0.1Three reasons to want it

They arrived independently, decades apart, and they have almost nothing to do with each other.

Never write the inverse twice

An enormous amount of software comes in pairs that must agree exactly. Encrypt and decrypt. Compress and decompress. Write a file format and read it back. Convert an incoming message into your own format and convert it back out. A database migration and its rollback. In every one of those, somebody writes both halves by hand and then has to keep them in step forever — and when they drift, the failure is silent and expensive: what went in does not come out. If the language derives one half from the other, they cannot drift.

Heat

Landauer, 1961: erasing one bit of information necessarily releases a definite minimum quantity of heat. Not as an engineering shortcoming — necessarily, as thermodynamics. Every logic gate that throws information away is paying it. Bennett, 1973: therefore a computation that never erases anything has, in principle, no minimum energy cost at all. Chips today run thousands of times above that floor, and have been approaching it for decades; data centres now consume a noticeable share of national electricity, almost all of it leaving as heat.

Physics

The laws governing an isolated quantum system supply only reversible operations — there is no "erase this" among them. So a quantum programming language has no choice about being a reversible one. This is the subject of the companion document, and it is the reason a piece of work begun for entirely different purposes turned out to matter.

The first reason is the immediate and unromantic one, and it is the one this particular language actually came from. Its ancestor was built for machine translation: grammars that had to run in both directions, turning a sentence into a meaning and a meaning back into a sentence, where writing the two halves separately and hoping they agreed was not an acceptable answer.

0.2Why it is hard: ordinary programs destroy things

Here is the obstacle, and it is bigger than it first sounds. Destroying information is most of what ordinary programs do.

  • Add one to a counter, and the old value is gone.
  • Add an item to a running total, and the item is unrecoverable.
  • Take a branch, and afterwards nothing records which branch you took.
  • Overwrite a field, discard a working value, delete a row.

The smallest example is addition. Two pallets and three pallets make five. Now look only at the five: were there two and three? One and four? Nothing in the answer says. Plain addition is not a reversible step, and neither is almost anything else you would normally write.

But look at the standard repair, because the entire field lives inside it. Rather than turning (a, b) into a+b, turn it into (a, a+b) — keep one of the inputs alongside the answer:

Nothing was lost, and you paid for it in the size of what you carry. That is the shape of every reversible design ever proposed: compute whatever you like, but carry enough with you to retrace the route. The whole field is an argument about what you are allowed to write given that constraint — and this paper's contribution is to notice that the constraint has been drawn tighter than it needs to be.

0.3Why this language looks so strange: it has no variables

You are about to see procedures written like this:

def double = copy ; add

and it is worth knowing in advance that there are no names in this language at all. Not "few". None.

Nearly all programming names things: put the count in x, put the weight in y, work out x times y. The names are the whole idiom. Here you never name a value. You describe the plumbing — what is connected to what. It is the difference between a written procedure with a clipboard and a conveyor schematic. The line above says: send the goods through a duplicator, then through an adder. There is no x and no y because there is nowhere to put one.

That is not an aesthetic preference, and this is the point worth carrying into §1. Look at what the other languages in this field actually struggle with:

Did you use that variable exactly once? Did you use it on both sides? Did you use it twice? Did you fail to use it at all?

Those are the questions that make one of the six languages reject a perfectly sensible program for using a name twice, and another for not using one at all, and force a third to carve out a special exception so that duplication is possible at all. They are all questions about names. With no names, none of them can even be asked, and the obligation the paper is about has nowhere to attach itself.

0.5So what is the paper actually arguing?

That the middle row is a mistake, and that the bottom row is both simpler and larger.

Everyone in the middle row is buying the same thing — a guarantee that running backwards gives one definite answer — and every one of them pays for it. One spends an extra built-in operation. One rejects programs people expect to write. One makes the meaning of a procedure depend on the order the rules are written in. One makes the programmer supply an extra correctness condition at every branch, and leaves the program undefined if they get it wrong.

This paper declines to buy the guarantee. Running backwards may return a shortlist. In exchange, reversal becomes something that always works, needs no checks, cannot fail, and rejects no program — and the reversal routine in the working implementation is eight lines long.

Everything after this is the case for that trade: what the others pay (§3.1), what declining buys (§3.2), what it costs (§3.3), why the trade is favourable for a structural reason rather than by luck (§3.4) — and then, in §5, a proof that nothing at all was given up in expressive power.

From here on, the paper.

AbstractThe argument in one page

A reversible procedure is one you can run backwards. If you want backward runs to give a single definite answer, you have to insist that no two of your rules ever produce the same result — otherwise, seeing that result, you cannot tell which rule you came through. Every existing language in this field insists on it, and they differ mainly in how. One assumes it and leaves the checking for later. One makes it the single rule the programmer must maintain by hand. One replaces it with an ordering, first match wins. One decides it mechanically. Two write it into the rules that say when a program is well formed. One makes the programmer supply an extra test at every branch.

This paper asks the question none of them asks: what if you simply do not insist?

The answer is a language in which running backwards may return several candidate origins rather than one. That is the entire cost, and it is paid in exactly one place. What it buys is that reversal becomes free and infallible: the reverse of a procedure is obtained by reversing each step and doing them in the opposite order, there is nothing to check, nothing that can fail, and no program that has to be rejected. The reversal routine in the working implementation is eight lines long.

The paper then does three more things. It gives the manifest system — the part that checks that goods and procedures fit together — which requires no annotations at all: it works out every manifest by itself, including the self-referential ones. It proves that the manifest system is sound, in a form that necessarily talks about both directions at once, for a reason that turns out to be the same reason reversal is free. And it settles exactly how expressive the language is: the procedures you can write are precisely the ones whose behaviour you could list out pair by pair — no more, and no less.

Section 1The obligation everybody accepts

Picture a dispatch hall. Consignments come in, a set of written procedures acts on them, consignments go out. Now you are asked to make the hall auditable in reverse: given anything on the outbound dock, you must be able to say what came in.

There is an obstruction, and it is completely obvious once stated. Suppose two different procedures can both produce an identical outbound pallet. Then that pallet does not tell you which procedure ran, and so it does not tell you what came in. If you want reverse audit to give one answer, you must forbid that situation: no two procedures may produce overlapping results.

Every language in this field carries that obligation. What is striking is how differently they discharge it, and that each way of discharging it costs something.

Six answers

Inv assumes it. Its authors require that when you offer a choice between two procedures, "not only the domains, but the ranges" must be disjoint — both what they accept and what they produce — and add candidly that "the disjointness may be checked by a type system, but we have not explored this possibility". The condition costs them an extra built-in operation: an inequality test exists in their language specifically because it is "sometimes necessary for ensuring the disjointness of the two branches of a union". In warehouse terms they had to add a "these two are not the same" check to the standard equipment list, purely to keep the audit trail clean.

Theseus makes it the one rule the programmer must maintain, and states it on both sides:

Non-overlapping and exhaustive coverage in pattern clauses. The collections of patterns in the left-hand side of each clause must be a complete non-overlapping covering of the input type. Similarly, the collections of patterns in the right-hand side of each clause must also be a complete non-overlapping covering of the return type.

Read as the rules must between them handle every possible incoming consignment; no two may accept the same one; and — the unusual half — the same must hold for everything they send out.

RFUN identifies the same problem, calling it "the functional variant of the problem of the general irreversibility of if-then-else constructs", and answers it not by forbidding overlap but by ordering it: results "must never match a branch that textually precedes it". First rule on the page wins. The audit becomes possible, but the meaning of the procedure now depends on the order the rules are written in.

PisoLang settles the question mechanically, by matching patterns against each other. Chardonnet, Lemonnier and Valiron write non-overlap into their formal rules as a side condition appearing twice, while deliberately dropping the requirement that the rules cover everything, "in order to allow non-terminating behaviour".

Janus — the one imperative language here, closer to ordinary step-by-step instructions — pushes the obligation onto the programmer. Every branch must carry a second test at the far end: "the predicate after if is the test, and that after fi is the assertion", and "the assertion makes the conditional reversible". The price is stated just as plainly: "if the assertion does not have the required value, execution of the loop is undefined". In warehouse terms, every fork in the process must be stamped at the exit with a note saying which way it came — and if the stamp is wrong, the procedure has no defined meaning at all.

Six languages, six answers, and one question none of them asks: what if the condition is simply not imposed?

1.1What happens if you decline

Declining it means moving from a world where each result has at most one origin to a world where it may have several. The reverse audit stops returning an answer and starts returning a shortlist. Written down, the law the language runs on becomes:

    x is a possible input for y   <=>   y is a possible output for x

    (in the paper:  x ∈ P(y)  <=>  y ∈ inv(P)(x) )

The word doing the work is "possible". Run forwards, then run the reverse, and you get back a list containing where you started rather than where you started. That is the price. It is paid in exactly one place, and the paper states it at the outset because the rest of the paper is an argument that it is worth paying.

What it buys is that reversal becomes total — defined for every procedure, with no conditions, no checks, no proof obligations and no undecidable questions anywhere. The rule is the one you would guess: to undo "load the truck, then seal it", you unseal it, then unload it. Reverse each step, and do the steps in the opposite order. That is the whole algorithm, and here it is, as it actually appears in the working code:

Only one line is interesting: the one for "do this, then that", where the two are swapped. All the others just push the reversal inwards. And reversing twice gets you the original text back exactly — not something equivalent to it, the same text.

Because reversal always works, the language does not even need to keep it around. When you write "reverse of (copy, then merge)", the reader — this document's word for the machinery that takes a written procedure and works out what it does — rewrites it on the spot into "reverse of merge, then reverse of copy", and the reversal symbol never appears again anywhere.

This property has been named three times independently, which is worth recording. Theseus calls an operation syntactically reversible when reversing its text gives you its reverse meaning. Yokoyama and Glück call an inversion local when "for any given program unit the inversion can always produce an inverse unit", and prove Janus has it. And the 1993 design this language descends from (§1.3) writes it as an equation. All three use "do this, then that" as the worked example, and all three arrive at the same order-swapping rule.

Where they differ is what it costs to hold the property everywhere. Janus holds it, and pays with the exit stamp on every branch — a thing the programmer must get right, whose failure mode is a procedure with no defined meaning. Theseus does not hold it: its own branching construct fails the property, and so does its yes/no test, which leaves open whether restricting tests to a well-behaved subclass costs you expressive power. The 1993 design does not hold it either, for the same reason.

This paper's language holds it with neither price: nothing for the programmer to discharge, and nothing excluded. The trick is that there is no branching construct to fail the property and no test operator to weaken. Branching is done by a general-purpose "handle bin one this way, bin two that way" operation, and filtering is assembled out of copying (§5.3).

1.2What the paper contributes

  1. A reversible language in which every single operation reverses by text alone (§2, §3). The paper shows this is exactly what dropping the obligation buys, and that the resulting theory is smaller than that of the languages which keep it, not larger.
  2. A manifest system that requires no annotations (§4), in which a self-referential manifest is accepted precisely when something could actually satisfy it. The standard check that normally rejects such manifests becomes a diagnosis instead of a failure. The paper proves the system sound, and shows the statement has to talk about both directions at once.
  3. An exact measure of expressive power (§5): the procedures you can write are precisely those whose behaviour is listable. Being able to compute anything computable is the easy half; the informative half is the ceiling, and it holds because every operation in the language is positive — nothing anywhere can express "this does not happen".

1.3Where it comes from

The design is not new. It is the second version of a language specified in a 1993 Master's thesis at the University of Amsterdam and never published — and it did not come from reversible computing at all. It came from machine translation: grammars that had to run in both directions, turning a sentence into a meaning and a meaning back into a sentence. That thesis already states the law above as a numbered equation, already defines reversal-by-text as an equation, and already notices that its own branching construct breaks it. What it does not have is the small forced set of basic operations described in §2. The lineage is recorded for honesty about priority; nothing in the paper rests on it.

Section 2The language

2.1What you can write

Start with manifests — the descriptions of what kind of thing may be handled. There are only four ways to build one, and then one way to make one refer to itself:

The self-referential ones are equirecursive, which means: "a stack" and "either empty or a box on a stack" are the same manifest, not two manifests that happen to correspond. Nothing has to be done to move between them — no unwrapping paperwork. That choice turns out to save a lot of machinery later.

Actual goods are built from the same four ideas: a token, "in the left bin, containing this", "in the right bin, containing this", and "these two together".

A procedure is built from thirteen basic steps and six ways of combining them. The thirteen are not a wishlist. They are exactly the steps that say the four manifest builders behave the way arithmetic behaves — that "either A or B" doesn't care about order, that grouping doesn't matter, that a token adds nothing, and so on — plus the two steps that make this a world of shortlists rather than a world of exact correspondences.

Those thirteen fall into three groups, and the three groups are exactly the three rows of the dial in §0.4 — which is worth noticing, because it means the design question of the whole field is settled by which of these you allow.

Eight are pure rearrangement

Everything from id down to dist. Nothing is created, destroyed or obscured; each definite consignment goes to exactly one other, and each has an obvious undo. Backwards, always exactly one answer. The top row of the dial.

Four may come up empty

The never-applies step, the two bin operations, and copying. Never ambiguous backwards, but sometimes no answer at all: "take it out of the left bin" has nothing to say about something in the right bin, and copying cannot be undone if the two copies differ. The middle row.

One can be ambiguous

Tipping the two bins into one — and that is the entire list. It is the only step in the language that can give two answers backwards, which is why §2.3 can say that all ambiguity anywhere traces to this one place. The bottom row.

Copying and bin-merging are also the pair §6 returns to, for a striking reason: the best-known language in the strict world explicitly names exactly these two as the things that cannot be done, and recovers them only through extra machinery.

2.2Checking that things fit

The manifest rules say when a procedure is well formed. They are exactly what you would write down yourself.

Two remarks. There are no rules for wrapping and unwrapping self-referential manifests, and their absence is the equirecursive choice from §2.1: a stack and its unfolding are the same manifest, so nothing needs to be written to pass between them.

And the last rule — the one for running backwards — is the law of the language showing up at the level of manifests. It swaps the two sides and does nothing else. In the implementation it is literally a swap.

2.3What the steps actually mean

The meaning of a procedure is a table — a ledger, if you prefer, which is what the companion document calls it — of which inbound goods may lead to which outbound goods. The evaluator does not consult manifests at all. If you hand a procedure something of the wrong shape, that is not an error — the procedure simply has nothing to do with it, and the table of possibilities for that input is empty. Being outside a procedure's scope is not a failure; it is having no outcome.

Look at inl and inr. Forwards they always work: you can always put something in the left bin. Backwards they may not: if the thing is in the right bin, "take it out of the left bin" has nothing to say. That is where the possibility of no answer enters the language.

Now look at join reversed. A pallet came out of the pile; which bin had it been in? Either. Two answers. And here is the fact worth carrying away from this section:

The single source of ambiguity

join run backwards is the only step anywhere in the language that can give more than one answer. Every shortlist longer than one item, in any program however large, traces back to a reversed merge.

That is a strong structural statement. Ambiguity in this language is not diffuse; it has exactly one origin, and you can point at it.

A procedure may be defined in terms of itself, and several may be defined in terms of each other. The meaning of such a definition is the obvious one: start from "nothing is related to anything", apply the definitions once, then again, then again, and collect everything that shows up at any finite stage. This works because every operation in the language only ever adds possibilities — including reversal, which just swaps every pair in the table — so the stages only grow, and the limit is well defined.

Finally, a procedure may take another procedure as a parameter — "controlled-by", say, which turns any step into a step that happens only if a flag is set. This works one level deep only: a parameter stands for a procedure, never for another parameter-taking procedure. Parameters are filled in before anything runs. §6 notes that two of the other languages make the same choice and describe it in the same words.

2.4This is not just route patterns

"Do this then that", "either this or that", "repeat this" are the three operations of a well-known small algebra — the same algebra behind text search patterns and behind simple route descriptions. The resemblance is not accidental; the 1993 design cites exactly that literature. But there are two differences, and they are what the paper is about.

First, that algebra deals in one kind of thing. Here every procedure is labelled with what it accepts and what it produces, and these may be different kinds, and the "two bins" and "travelling together" operations act on those labels rather than being extra operations on a single pool. Second, the basic steps are not an arbitrary chosen alphabet; they are forced, being exactly the statements that the four manifest builders behave like arithmetic.

So the setting is route patterns over forced data plumbing, and the content is in the interaction. dist is what lets the route operations see inside a two-bin choice. copy and join are what let them leave the world of exact correspondence.

One consequence matters later. If the manifest allows only finitely many possible consignments, then "then", "either" and "repeat" between them give you no more power than a fixed flowchart. All of the open-endedness in the language comes from the self-referential manifests, never from the control structure.

Section 3What dropping the condition buys

3.1What it costs those who accept it

§1 listed six ways of discharging one obligation. What is less often noted is that each one charges a fee.

  • Inv spends a piece of standard equipment: an inequality test that exists only to establish non-overlap.
  • Theseus must reject procedures a working programmer would expect to write. Its own paper lists four invalid examples, of which one merely fails to use something it was given, and another uses something twice. In warehouse terms: throwing an item away and duplicating an item are both forbidden outright.
  • PisoLang must decide non-overlap mechanically, and must then carve out a special exception to get duplication back at all.
  • Chardonnet et al. carry non-overlap as two side conditions inside a formal rule.
  • RFUN must fix an order on its rules and make the meaning depend on it.
  • Janus pushes the obligation onto the programmer, who must supply a correct exit stamp at every branch, and whose procedure has no defined meaning if the stamp is wrong.

Janus repays a closer look, because on one point it agrees with this paper against all the functional languages. It does not require its operations to have unique origins. Its authors write that evaluating an expression "is not backward deterministic because the function is not injective, and thus there exists no inverse", and then that "this does not harm the backward and forward determinism of Janus statements". Uniqueness is demanded of statements, not of arithmetic, and a restriction on how you may write an assignment buys it. So Janus and this paper agree that non-unique operations are admissible. They differ only in where the bill is sent: Janus to a restriction plus a stamp on every branch, 42 to the shortlist.

3.2What declining it buys

Reversal always works

The reverse of a procedure is defined for every procedure, by the eight lines of §1.1. There is no case where it is undefined, no condition to check, and no property of the input that must be established first. Reversing twice returns the original text exactly.

Reversal need not be kept

Because it always works, the reader pushes it down to the individual steps and discards it. No part of the machinery — evaluator, printer, checker, optimiser — ever meets a reversed composite procedure.

Nothing can get stuck

In a language of exact correspondences, a procedure that is well formed but has nothing to do is an embarrassment: it means a guarantee you wanted to make is false. PisoLang records exactly such a case. Here, a procedure with nothing to do is a perfectly ordinary procedure that relates nothing, and no guarantee is lost because none was claimed.

Ambiguity has one source

Every shortlist longer than one traces to a reversed merge (§2.3). A language that forbids ambiguity must exclude it everywhere; a language that permits it can point at it.

3.3What it costs

The shortlist

Run forwards, then backwards, and you get a list containing where you started rather than where you started. That is the whole of the cost.

Here it is, in the smallest possible example. A procedure that adds two counts:

Five pallets could have been two-and-three, or one-and-four, or any of six splits. Both directions run the same written definition; the backward one is its mechanical reverse. Whether that shortlist is acceptable is a real question about what you want a reversible procedure for — and the paper immediately notes something that softens it considerably: combining steps can restore the uniqueness that an individual step lacks.

Take "make a copy of the count, then add the two copies" — a doubling procedure. Run it backwards from 6. The addition offers all seven splits of 6, but the copy, run backwards, keeps only the ones where both halves are identical. Exactly one survives: 3. And run it backwards from 7 and none survives, correctly, because 7 is not double anything. The shortlist has collapsed to a single answer, without anyone imposing a condition.

3.4Why the trade is favourable in principle

There is a reason the theory gets smaller rather than larger, and it is not luck.

§5 will show that the procedures expressible in this language are exactly the ones whose behaviour is listable: you could print out all the valid input/output pairs, one after another, forever. Now ask what the reverse of such a procedure looks like. You take the listing and swap each pair. That is all. Reversal is free because the class of listable things is obviously closed under swapping.

The languages that keep the obligation live in a smaller class — behaviours where every output has at most one input. That class is closed under reversal too, but the closure has to be earned: every construct the language admits must be checked to preserve uniqueness, and that checking is precisely the obligation. So:

The point of the whole paper

Reversal being free in the syntax is a shadow of reversal being free in the underlying world. The languages that must check things are the ones whose underlying world does not hand them reversal for nothing.

Section 4Manifests

Manifests are worked out, never written. Nothing in what the programmer types mentions one, and there is no wrapping or unwrapping to do.

4.1Working them out

The method is the standard one from ordinary programming languages: collect the constraints each step imposes and solve them. It is unusually easy here, because every definition stands on its own — there are no local variables anywhere in the language — so none of the usual complications arise. Groups of mutually recursive definitions are solved together and generalised once.

A self-referential manifest is never something the programmer asks for. It is what the checker discovers when the constraints close a loop:

def double = copy ; add      -->  double : count <-> count

Nobody declared "count" anywhere. The copy forces the two halves of the addition to agree, and that constraint, solved, is "a count in, a count out".

4.2A loop is the self-referential manifest

Where a conventional checker finds a manifest referring to itself, declares it impossible and stops, this one accepts the loop and carries on. Comparing two manifests then has to work "up to unwrapping": assume the two are the same, and check that the assumption survives one layer of unwrapping. This terminates for a concrete reason — a looping manifest, however deeply you unwrap it, only ever has finitely many distinct parts, so the set of assumptions cannot keep growing.

4.3But a loop is read before it is trusted

Not every loop describes something real. Compare two:

“A stack is empty, or a box on a stack”

Fine. There are things that satisfy it: the empty stack, one box, two boxes. The loop has a way out.

“A crate contains two crates”

Nothing whatsoever satisfies this. Every crate would need two crates inside it, forever. No finite object qualifies.

The test is one unwrapping with the self-reference taken to be empty:

The test

A self-referential manifest describes something real exactly when it describes something real after you replace the self-reference by "nothing qualifies".

If it comes out empty at that first step, it will be empty at every step. So the check that other languages use to reject looping manifests becomes, here, a diagnosis: a loop is admitted when something can satisfy it and rejected when nothing can, and the rejection message names the specific constraint that could not be satisfied. The paper knows of no other language in this family that works its recursive manifests out at all — in all of them they are declared by hand.

4.4Soundness, and why it must face both ways

What should the manifest system guarantee? The obvious answer: if you feed a procedure something matching its input manifest, whatever comes out matches its output manifest. That is the guarantee every such system aims for, and here it cannot be proved. The reason is worth following, because it is the same reason reversal is free.

Proofs of this kind go by working through the procedure's structure one operation at a time, assuming the guarantee for the parts and deriving it for the whole. Now reach the operation "run backwards". For a reversed procedure, the thing you need to conclude about is what the original procedure accepts — and the assumption you were handed only says something about what the original produces. The proof has nothing to work with.

The fix is to state the guarantee symmetrically from the start.

Proposition 1  ·  Soundness

Suppose a procedure is checked as going from manifest A to manifest B. Then for any pair of goods it relates: if either end matches its manifest, both do.

Stated that way the guarantee is self-mirroring: the claim about a procedure going A to B and the claim about its reverse going B to A are word for word the same sentence. So the troublesome case is discharged by observing that reversing a procedure just swaps every pair in its table, and the condition does not care which way round a pair is written.

The moral

A language whose reversal always works cannot have a one-directional soundness guarantee — and does not need one. The law of the language has reappeared inside the proof.

How the proof goes

By working through each operation. The thirteen basic steps are immediate from Figure 3, and three are representative.

  • Merge. A pair it relates is "left-bin-x to x" or "right-bin-x to x". And x is a legitimate two-bin item exactly when x is a legitimate item — the both-ways statement the guarantee wants, for free.
  • Left bin. A pair is "x to left-bin-x", and left-bin-x is legitimate exactly when x is.
  • Dropping a dead bin. A pair is "right-bin-x to x", and the manifest "either nothing-qualifies or A" has no left-bin members at all, because nothing qualifies for the left. Which is precisely why the evaluator returns no outcome when handed a left-bin item — the implementation and the proof agree.

For "do this then that": you have goods going in, something in the middle, goods coming out. If you know the front end is legitimate, use the first assumption to get the middle, then the second to get the far end. If instead you know the far end is legitimate, use the same two assumptions in the other order. Both readings are available precisely because the guarantee is two-directional.

The two-bin and travelling-together cases reduce to the parts. Note that "travelling together" already needs the two-way form even with no reversal in sight: knowing only that the outgoing pair is legitimate means reading each assumption backwards. "Either procedure" applies whichever assumption fits. "Repeat" is the identity at zero steps, and each further step is the assumption for the body, read in whichever direction the known end supplies. Reversal is as above. And for a self-referential definition, the meaning is the union of its finite stages (§2.3); each stage has the property, and the property is about individual pairs, so it survives the union.

Corollary 2

Feed a checked procedure something matching its input manifest and every answer it gives matches the output manifest. Run it backwards from something matching the output manifest and every answer matches the input manifest.

Note what this does not say. Nothing here promises that there is an answer. "Take one away" applied to zero gives nothing at all, which is a perfectly legitimate outcome, so there is no liveness guarantee to state and none is lost (§3.2).

Section 5Exactly how much it can express

5.1The statement

First, the word "listable". A behaviour — a table of which inputs may lead to which outputs — is listable if you could write a program that prints its entries one after another, forever, such that every genuine entry appears eventually. Note what you do not get: if an entry is not in the table, no amount of waiting tells you so. You just keep waiting. This is the standard notion of "as much as a computer can do" for tables that may be infinite.

Theorem 3

Fix any two manifests. A behaviour between them can be written as a program in this language exactly when it is listable.

Two remarks on why the paper states this, rather than the usual "the language can compute anything computable" — which is only the easier half.

First, the harder half is the ceiling, and it is the more informative one. It says the language expresses exactly the listable behaviours and nothing beyond: no program in it decides questions that no computer can decide, and no amount of adding new basic steps can change that without adding something the language deliberately does not have (see §5.2).

Second, the statement has the right shape. The reference result in this field is that reversible machines compute exactly the computable behaviours with unique origins; and one of the six languages proves the matching statement for itself, concluding that it "fully characterises all of the computable morphisms" of its world. Read side by side, that result and Theorem 3 are the same sentence in two different worlds. What this language changes is not the theorem; it is the world the theorem is read in.

5.2The ceiling

Proposition 4

Every procedure written in this language has listable behaviour, and the listing program can be produced mechanically from the text.

Three steps. The thirteen basic steps have behaviour you can decide outright by looking. Each of the six combinations preserves listability: "then" by working on many partial answers in rotation so none starves; "either" by alternating between the two listings; the two bin operations by tagging or pairing; reversal by swapping each printed pair; and "repeat" because the n-step behaviour is uniformly listable in n. Self-reference adds nothing new: the meaning is the union of the finite stages, a listing for each stage can be produced from the stage number, and the same rotation trick collects the whole.

Corollary 5

No program in this language expresses a behaviour beyond the listable ones.

Where the ceiling comes from

Not where you would guess. Not from reversibility, and not from any restriction on recursion — the language has fully general recursion. It comes from the shape of what you can write:

The reason

Every operation in the language is positive. Nothing anywhere expresses a negative. There is no way to write "this step fails here".

A hypothetical extra operation meaning "t does not apply to this" would describe the complement of a scope, and would break the ceiling immediately. The practical consequence is a licence: you may add any number of new basic steps to the language, as long as each is decidable by inspection, and the theorem survives untouched.

It is a small pleasure that the 1993 design also excluded negation, for a related but distinct reason: that "everything except this finite list" cannot be written down finitely when the universe of possible goods is infinite. Convergent instinct, different argument.

5.3Four things you can build

The other half of the theorem — that everything listable can be written — is assembled from four constructions. None of them is a new feature; each is built out of what §2 already lists.

Throwing something away

Lemma 6  ·  Discard

For any manifest there is a procedure taking anything of that kind to a bare token — reducing it to "something was here" and nothing more.

It is built by following the manifest's own structure: for a two-bin choice, reduce each bin and merge; for a pair, reduce both halves and drop the resulting token; and so on, with a self-referential definition for the self-referential manifests.

This deserves a comment, because it looks like a contradiction. Throwing information away is supposedly the one thing a reversible language must not do — and there is no such basic step in the thirteen. Yet it is definable. There is no contradiction, because the actual rule was never "you may not throw anything away". The rule is:

The real rule

If a step loses information, then running it backwards must be allowed to offer every candidate. Reduce a consignment to a bare token, then run that backwards, and out comes the entire catalogue of consignments that could have produced it.

This language forbids forgetting reversibly. It does not forbid forgetting.

For contrast: Theseus lists exactly this program among its invalid expressions.

Relating everything to everything

Lemma 7  ·  The catalogue

"Reduce to a token, then run a reduction backwards" relates every possible input to every possible output.

Which sounds useless and is in fact the engine of the whole proof. It is a generator of possibilities: hand it anything and it offers the complete catalogue of the other kind. Keep it in mind for §5.5.

Filtering

Lemma 8  ·  Filter

Take any test — any procedure that succeeds on some consignments and has no outcome on the rest. Then "copy the consignment, submit one copy to the test, and pass the other copy through unchanged" is a procedure that lets exactly the accepted consignments through, untouched.

Copy the docket, send one copy for approval, and if approval comes back the other copy proceeds. If approval never comes, nothing proceeds. This is how a test that might never answer becomes a usable step.

It also answers a question Theseus left open (§1.1). Filtering here is not a construct that had to be designed; it is a three-part assembly. It is its own reverse for any test whatsoever, and no restriction to well-behaved tests is needed.

Writing anything down as a barcode

Lemma 9  ·  Serialisation

For any manifest there is a procedure encoding any consignment of that kind as a string of bits, with two properties: different consignments get different strings, and the set of valid strings is prefix-free — no valid code is the beginning of another valid code.

Prefix-freeness is the barcode property: you can tell where one code ends without being told its length. It is built the same way as the discard, by following the manifest's structure: a two-bin choice becomes a leading 0 or 1 followed by the encoding of the contents, and a pair becomes the two encodings joined end to end.

That last clause looks unsound and is not, and the reason is a lovely one. "Joined end to end", run backwards, gives you every way of splitting the string — six ways, for the five-bit encoding of a particular pair of counts. And yet the full encoding of a pair, run backwards, gives exactly one answer, because prefix-freeness rules out every split except the one that actually decodes. This is the phenomenon from §3.3 — combining steps restoring uniqueness that an individual step lacks — doing real load-bearing work inside a proof.

5.4Simulating a machine

Lemma 10  ·  Halting

For any deterministic machine of the standard theoretical kind, there is a procedure in this language that succeeds on exactly the inputs on which that machine eventually stops, and has no outcome on the others.

The standard theoretical machine is easy to picture as a warehouse. There is one very long aisle of bays, each bay either empty or holding a marker. A picker stands at one bay. The picker holds an instruction card. Reading what is in the current bay, the card tells the picker what to leave in the bay, whether to step left or right, and which card to pick up next. That is all a computer is, in theory, and it is enough for anything computable.

The encoding

A snapshot of the aisle is written as three parts: the bays to the left in reverse order, the bay you are standing at, and the bays to the right. And the instruction card is a label on the whole snapshot, not a fourth part of it. That single decision is what keeps the whole simulation short. Because the card is a label — a bin, in the sense of §2 — acting differently under different cards is just "handle bin one this way, bin two that way", which is already an operation in the language. Nothing has to reach in, extract the card, consult it, and put it back.

Stepping right is: push the current bay onto the left list, pop the next bay off the right list. Written out it is one line. And stepping left is not written out at all — it is "stepping right, backwards". Likewise, reading the current bay is one line, and writing to it is that line run backwards. This is the paper's thesis showing up as an economy of about half the code.

The word doing the work is repeat. "Repeat one step, any number of times" gives you the entire run of the machine. The halting card is a dead end, so once the machine stops, nothing further is reachable — and "select the halt label" therefore picks out the halted snapshot, of which there is at most one.

The one case worth keeping in full

An aisle in theory is infinite; a written record is finite. So the encoding is many-to-one: trailing empty bays are represented by not being mentioned. When the picker steps right off the end of the written record, a special branch invents an empty bay — and this is correct precisely because an empty right-hand list means the next bay is empty.

Note what the simulation claim carefully does not say. It says nothing about the record being in a canonical form. Hand it a record with three trailing empties written out explicitly and it gives you back a record with three trailing empties, and since the claim is about what the record describes rather than how it is written, the padding simply rides along. The whole finite-versus-infinite mismatch costs one branch of one definition and one case of one proof.

A working instance of this construction — a three-card machine that adds one to a binary number — is included with the implementation and runs.

5.5Everything listable can be written

Theorem 11

For any two manifests and any listable behaviour between them, some program in this language expresses exactly that behaviour.

The proof is a construction, and the shape of it is the point. Given the behaviour you want:

  1. Encode input-and-output-together as a barcode (Lemma 9). The set of valid barcodes for the behaviour you want is listable.
  2. Take a machine that stops on exactly those barcodes, and turn it into a test (Lemma 10). The test succeeds on exactly the input/output pairs you want to allow.
  3. Turn that test into a filter (Lemma 8).
  4. And now the trick: guess the answer, then check it.

Follow a consignment through. Copy gives you two of it. Catalogue replaces the second copy by every possible outcome, in turn (Lemma 7). Filter keeps only those pairings that the behaviour actually allows. Discard the input throws away the first copy, which has done its job. What is left is the outcome. So the procedure expresses exactly the behaviour you wanted.

Why an injective language cannot do this

The guess is "reduce to a token, then run a reduction backwards" — a step with many answers by design. The check is "make a copy" — whose reverse keeps only matching pairs. Both are already in the language; neither was invented for the proof. A language that insists every result have a unique origin cannot write the guess at all.

Corollary 12  ·  Full computational power

Every computable function whatsoever is expressible — that is just the special case where the behaviour happens to allow one outcome per input. And by Proposition 4, nothing worse is expressible.

Section 6The neighbours

One dial orders the whole field

How many answers may a backward run give? That single question sorts every language in the area.

Almost the entire field is the middle row. Reversible languages are, with near unanimity, uniqueness languages, differing in surface syntax and in how uniqueness is enforced rather than in what they can express.

Inv

The nearest relative, and it deserves generosity. Its point-free relational setting is this setting; its reversal law is this law; and its observation that undoing a duplication requires an equality check — "it takes a pair and lets it go through only if the two components are equal" — is exactly the observation this paper makes about reversed copying, made twenty years earlier. The difference is that Inv restricts itself to unique-origin behaviours and this language does not.

The Π branch

Π is built from precisely the statements that the manifest builders behave like arithmetic — which is Figure 1 minus the last two rows. And the two missing rows are the interesting part. Having listed its operations, the Π literature explicitly names two things that are not among them, and 42's copy and join are exactly witnesses for one of them and its mirror image. Π does get them back — but only in a separate supervisory language, as information effects built from "create" and "erase", where merging two bins is spelled out as "convert the input into a pair whose first component records which bin, then erase that component".

The comparison in one sentence

What Π must treat as a special effect — requiring an effect-tracking system and a supervisory language on top — this language has as an ordinary basic step, because in a world of shortlists those two operations are perfectly ordinary steps.

Two smaller differences: Π makes you write explicit wrapping and unwrapping for self-referential manifests, where the equirecursive choice of §2.1 costs nothing; and one of Π's basic steps is absent here, being redundant — "an impossible thing travelling with something else" is itself impossible, so it is already covered by the step that never applies.

Procedures as parameters

Theseus describes its parameterised procedures as "a macro or a meta-language construction", stating plainly that "Theseus does not have high-order maps in the formal sense". RFUN says its procedure parameters are "not enough to make functions first class citizens". This paper's parameters are the same choice, made independently and for the same reason. Two of the six languages do go further and nest procedure parameters freely.

Program inversion

The classical ancestor, and a genuinely old tradition: Dijkstra, Gries, and others on the question of how to invert a given program — including a famous exercise on inverting a tree traversal. Where that tradition inverts a particular program by hand and by cleverness, this language makes the inverse fall out of the text automatically.

Janus

The imperative ancestor, and the only prior language the paper knows of in which every construct reverses by text alone. Janus buys that with the exit stamp; this paper buys it by admitting shortlists. Those two prices are the real subject of the comparison, and Janus's is the more familiar one: it is an obligation discharged per program, whose failure mode is a procedure with undefined behaviour rather than a procedure that gets rejected.

Section 7Conclusion

The paper presents a reversible language that declines the uniqueness its neighbours pay for, and argues the trade is favourable. Reversal becomes total. The reversal symbol leaves the machinery entirely. No liveness guarantee is at risk, because none was claimed. Ambiguity has a single, nameable source. And the price is one word: possible.

It gives a manifest system that needs no annotations, including for the self-referential manifests — which it works out, and then checks that something could actually satisfy. It proves that system sound in the two-directional form that a total reversal forces. And it settles expressive power exactly: the expressible behaviours are precisely the listable ones — the same theorem the uniqueness languages prove about themselves, read in a larger world.

Limitations, stated here rather than left to reviewers

  • Nothing is machine-checked; no proof assistant has seen these proofs.
  • Three standard simplifications of the theoretical machine are cited from the literature rather than re-proved.
  • "Repeat" is computed by brute-force accumulation, so it only finishes when the possibilities run out. A behaviour can be computable in one direction and not the other: "count down from here" finishes, "count up from here" does not.
  • Backward runs can be very slow. Nothing prunes the search.
  • A recursive definition is given a single manifest inside its own group, in the standard way.
  • Worked-out manifests are not in a canonical form, self-referential manifests having no unique way of being written down.
  • Parameters are filled in before anything runs, so a procedure that takes a parameter and refers to itself cannot be run, even though it checks out. The paper's example passes the manifest checker and its reverse is computed correctly, but filling in the parameter never terminates. Interpreting parameters as they are reached, rather than filling them in beforehand, would fix it, and nothing in the manifest system stands in the way.

Future work

That last limitation is the most inviting. Beyond it: the ceiling of §5 holds precisely because nothing in the language expresses a negative, which suggests a principled account of what admitting negation would actually cost.

And a sibling

Here is the part that sounds like a joke and is not. The meaning of a procedure in this language is a table of yes/no entries: can this input lead to that output, yes or no. Replace yes/no by ordinary numbers — the sort that can be negative, and can cancel each other out — and the very same tables become the operations of quantum computing. The same program text, the same reader, the same manifest checker, and literally the same reversal routine. Two of the six combinations have to go, being exactly the two whose meaning depends on "yes or yes" being the same as "yes". That is a separate paper, and it exists.

Artifact

A working implementation with no external dependencies, 368 tests, every result claimed in the manual re-run automatically by a checker, and the two figures of §2 verified against the implementation by the test suite.
